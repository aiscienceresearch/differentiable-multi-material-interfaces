\section{Test fields}
\label{sec:fields-supp}

Figure~\ref{fig:testfields} shows the five test fields of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. A sixth field, defined after the five, is the
enclosed cell of Table~\ref{tab:exact}. All six are defined on the box
$[-1,1]^3$, and each is a PyTorch module, so its parameters can receive
gradients from a loss on the extracted mesh. The $K$ regions of each field
fill the box; there is no background label.

\paragraph{Power diagram ($K=5$).} $f_k(x) = -\lVert x - c_k\rVert^2 + w_k$
with all weights $w_k = 0$, so the partition is the Voronoi diagram of the
sites $c_1 = (0.35, 0.20, 0.10)$, $c_2 = (-0.30, 0.25, -0.15)$,
$c_3 = (0.05, -0.40, 0.22)$, $c_4 = (-0.15, -0.10, 0.45)$ and
$c_5 = (0.10, 0.05, -0.48)$.

\paragraph{Sector fan ($K=4$).} $f_k(x) = \langle x - c, d_k\rangle$ with
center $c = (0.05, -0.02, 0.03)$ and $d_1,\dots,d_4$ the unit vectors from the
center of a regular tetrahedron to its corners. Since
$\langle x - c, d_k\rangle = \lVert x - c\rVert \cos\psi_k$, where $\psi_k$
is the angle between $x - c$ and $d_k$, each point takes the label of the
direction closest to it as seen from $c$, and each region is a cone with apex
$c$. The six interfaces are flat
wedges, which meet three at a time along four straight triple lines, and these
meet at one quadruple point at $c$. Every dihedral angle is $120^\circ$. The fan is
the Voronoi diagram of the four points $c + d_k$; unlike the five sites above,
these are arranged symmetrically, so that every region meets every other at
the single point $c$.

\paragraph{Spherical shells ($K=4$).} $f_k(x) = -(\lVert x\rVert - r_k)^2$ with
$r = (0.15, 0.45, 0.75, 1.05)$, so each point takes the label of the nearest
radius and the interface between shells $k$ and $k+1$ is the sphere of radius
$(r_k + r_{k+1})/2$, that is $0.30$, $0.60$ and $0.90$.

\paragraph{Neural fields ($K=4$ and $K=6$).} A neural
field~\cite{xie2022} is a network that
takes a point ${x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)}$ in 3D and returns its $K$ logits, so it defines $f$ like the
fields above, and the label at $x$ is the largest of its outputs. The point
is first mapped to Fourier features~\cite{tancik2020}. Each coordinate $x_i$ contributes itself
and $\sin(2^j x_i)$ and $\cos(2^j x_i)$ for $j = 0,\dots,3$, nine numbers per
coordinate and $27$ for the point. These are fixed functions of $x$ and add
no information, but they set the scale on which the field can vary, down to
the period $2\pi/8 \approx 0.79$ of $\sin(8 x_i)$ in a box of width $2$; on
the raw coordinates alone, an untrained network of this size gives nearly flat
interfaces, similar to those of a power diagram. The features feed a
multilayer perceptron with three hidden
layers of width $64$ ($K=4$) or $48$ ($K=6$), $\tanh$ activations and a
linear output of $K$ logits. The weights are drawn once from PyTorch's default
initialization, with seeds $0$ and $2$, and then held fixed; the networks are
not trained. At this initialization the logits sit at constant offsets larger
than their variation over the box, so that one or two labels would fill
almost all of it. The output layer is rescaled so that every logit
has mean zero and standard deviation one over a grid of $32^3$ points in the
box, after which each label fills between $13\%$ and $30\%$ of it. Sines,
cosines, weighted sums and $\tanh$ are all smooth in $x$, so the logits are
smooth, and so are the interfaces between labels. These fields have no
special structure, and their interfaces have no closed form.

\paragraph{Enclosed power-diagram cell ($K=15$).} The field of
Table~\ref{tab:exact} and Figure~\ref{fig:failure} is a power diagram with one
site at $(0.02, -0.01, 0.03)$ and $14$ around it, at $0.55$ times the eight
corners $(\pm1, \pm1, \pm1)$ of a cube and the six points $(\pm1.4, 0, 0)$,
$(0, \pm1.4, 0)$ and $(0, 0, \pm1.4)$, each moved by independent Gaussian
noise of standard deviation $0.06$. The central cell is a truncated cube with
$14$ faces and $24$ corners, entirely inside the box.

\section{Features per cell}
\label{sec:features-supp}

Section~\ref{sec:solve} classifies the vertices of the output by the number
of labels that tie at them and locates each by a small linear solve. This
section completes that count, works one solve through by hand, and measures
how often a cell carries more than one feature.

\subsection{Kinds of vertex}

A vertex of the output is a single point in 3D and is fixed by three
equations, one for each coordinate. With fewer equations the solution is not
a point; one equation gives a plane and two give a line. If $m$ labels tie, the
tie gives $m-1$ equations, since setting $m$ values equal takes $m-1$
equalities. The remaining $3-(m-1)$ must come from the tetrahedron $\tet$:
lying on one of its faces is one equation, the plane of that face, and lying
on an edge, where two faces meet, is two.

For two labels, the tie $\interp_k = \interp_l$ is a plane, which crosses
$\tet$ in a flat polygon, the interface patch if no other label is present.
Its corners, the crossings, are where the plane meets the edges of $\tet$.
For three labels the
tie is a line, the triple curve, which becomes a point where it pierces a face
of $\tet$. For four labels the tie is already a point and can lie anywhere
inside $\tet$. Five labels would need $3-4=-1$ equations from the
tetrahedron, which is impossible, so on a generic field at most four labels
tie at any point (Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}).
Table~\ref{tab:vertexkinds} summarizes the three cases.

\subsection{The linear system}

Equation~\eqref{eq:solve} is one small system per candidate tie, that is, per
entity and label set, and $m$ is fixed within it: $m=2$ on an edge, $3$ on a
face and $4$ in a tetrahedron. The equations contributed by $\tet$ never
appear as rows. They are satisfied by construction, because the point is
written in the barycentric coordinates $\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{m-1}$ of the
face of $\tet$ that has exactly $m$ nodes, so the coordinates of the other
nodes of $\tet$ are zero. These zeros are the $3-(m-1)$ equations from $\tet$.
The condition $\sum_a \lambda_a = 1$ removes one more unknown, which leaves the
$m-1$ unknowns $\lambda' = (\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{m-1})$ for the $m-1$ tie
equations, a square system.

As an example, take a triple point, $m = 3$, on a face with nodes
$v_0 = (0,0,0)$, $v_1 = (1,0,0)$ and $v_2 = (0,1,0)$, which lies in the plane
$z=0$; that plane is the one equation contributed by $\tet$. Let the tied
labels be $S = \{l_0, l_1, l_2\}$, and let the logits $L_a = f(v_a)$ at the
three nodes be
\[
\big(L_a[l_0],\, L_a[l_1],\, L_a[l_2]\big) =
\begin{cases}
(2,\, 0,\, 0) & a = 0,\\
(0,\, 3,\, 0) & a = 1,\\
(0,\, 0,\, 1) & a = 2.
\end{cases}
\]
These values are chosen only to keep the arithmetic short. Each node favors
one label, by $2$, $3$ and $1$, and gives the other two zero.
The differences $d_a^{\,r} = L_a[l_0] - L_a[l_r]$ are
$(d_0^{\,1}, d_1^{\,1}, d_2^{\,1}) = (2, -3, 0)$ for the pair $l_0, l_1$ and
$(d_0^{\,2}, d_1^{\,2}, d_2^{\,2}) = (2, 0, -1)$ for the pair $l_0, l_2$.
With $A_{ra} = d_a^{\,r} - d_0^{\,r}$ and $b_r = -d_0^{\,r}$,
Equation~\eqref{eq:solve} becomes
\[
\begin{pmatrix} -5 & -2 \\ -2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\lambda_1 = \tfrac{2}{11},\quad \lambda_2 = \tfrac{6}{11},\quad
\lambda_0 = 1 - \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 = \tfrac{3}{11}.
\]
All three coordinates are positive, so the tie lies inside the face and passes
the containment test. At this point the three interpolated logits are
$2\cdot\tfrac{3}{11} = 3\cdot\tfrac{2}{11} = 1\cdot\tfrac{6}{11} = \tfrac{6}{11}$,
equal as required, and the vertex is
$x = \sum_a \lambda_a v_a = (\tfrac{2}{11}, \tfrac{6}{11}, 0)$. It is emitted
if, in addition, no other label exceeds $\tfrac{6}{11}$ there, which is the
dominance test. Had one of the coordinates come out negative, the three labels
would tie outside the face, the configuration of
Figure~\ref{fig:multifeature} (right), and the face would carry no triple
point for this label set. A crossing ($m=2$) is the same computation with one
equation and one unknown, $t = \lambda_1$, and a quadruple point ($m=4$) with
three of each and no equation from $\tet$. The released implementation returns
the same values for this input.

Our method stores a vertex per entity \emph{and label set}, not per entity, so
an edge with several crossings carries several vertices. On each mixed
edge, one whose two endpoints have different labels, we solve the tie of every label pair and keep those that lie inside the
edge and are not beaten. On the edge of Figure~\ref{fig:multifeature} (left)
this keeps two crossings, one between labels $1$ and $3$ and one between $3$
and $0$. Labels $1$ and $0$ also tie on this edge, but at a point where label
$3$ is larger, so that tie is rejected. The two regions never meet on this
edge, although a single crossing between the endpoint labels would place a
wall between them there. The two kept crossings belong to different interfaces, and
since assembly groups vertices by tetrahedron and label pair, the first
becomes a corner of the $1|3$ patch and the second of the $3|0$ patch
in every tetrahedron around the edge. Each vertex is computed once per global
edge, so all of those tetrahedra share it. Faces with several triple points
are handled the same way, with one vertex per surviving label triple.

\section{Assembly}
\label{sec:assembly-supp}

This section gives the details of the assembly step of
Section~\ref{sec:assembly}.

\subsection{Patches and shared corners}

Assembly builds one patch per tetrahedron and label pair, a flat polygon in
the plane $\interp_k = \interp_l$. Triple curves and quadruple points are not
triangulated separately. The output surface consists of the places where two
labels tie, and the places where three or four regions meet appear as edges
and corners shared by these pairwise patches. A vertex becomes a corner of
every patch whose label pair is contained in its tied set. A
crossing between labels $1$ and $2$ is a corner of the $1|2$ patch only. A
triple point of $\{1,2,3\}$ is a corner of the three patches $1|2$, $1|3$ and
$2|3$, and the triple-curve segment between two such points is an edge of all
three. A quadruple
point of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ is a corner of all six patches of its tetrahedron.

Each shared corner is a single stored vertex, not a set of copies. The three
patches around a triple curve therefore join exactly along it, without gaps
or duplicated vertices, as the closure checks of
Section~\ref{sec:closure-supp} confirm, and moving such a vertex during
optimization moves all three patches together. The triple-curve segments that
the extractor also returns are a list of these shared edges, used to measure
curve length and to draw the curves; assembly does not use them.

\subsection{Dominance on emitted triangles}

Every corner of the $k|l$ patch has passed the dominance test against all $K$
labels, so at each corner $p$ we have $\interp_k(p) = \interp_l(p) \ge
\interp_j(p)$ for every other label $j$. Each triangle of the patch lies in
one tetrahedron, where $\interp_k - \interp_j$ is affine, and a point $x$ of
the triangle is a convex combination $x = \sum_i \gamma_i p_i$ of its corners, with
$\gamma_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i \gamma_i = 1$. An affine function takes such a combination
of points to the same combination of its values, so
\[
\interp_k(x) - \interp_j(x) = \sum_i \gamma_i \big(\interp_k(p_i) - \interp_j(p_i)\big) \ge 0,
\]
since every term is a nonnegative weight times a nonnegative difference. A
label that does not beat $k$ and $l$ at the corners therefore does not beat
them anywhere on the triangle, and checking the corners suffices. In floating
point the test lets a label exceed the tied pair by up to $10^{-12}$ at a
corner (Section~\ref{sec:predicates}), and the same sum bounds it by $10^{-12}$
on the whole triangle. Together with Propositions~\ref{prop:convex}
and~\ref{prop:complete} and Corollary~\ref{cor:fan}, this means that the fan
covers the true patch exactly, and since the regions partition the
tetrahedron, patches of different pairs meet only along their shared edges
and corners. If a label were missing from a cell's candidate set, which
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} excludes except for a region whose margin is
below the tolerance (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), a true corner would not
be solved and the patch would have a hole; but every corner that is emitted
has been tested against all $K$ labels, so the error is an omission and never
a triangle on which another label wins.

\subsection{Triangulation of a patch}

Inside a tetrahedron the interface between labels $k<l$ is a convex polygon
(Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}), but its corners arrive unordered, since they
are crossings on the edges, triple points on the faces and possibly a
quadruple point inside. The patch lies in the
plane $\interp_l - \interp_k = 0$, whose normal $n = \nabla(\interp_l -
\interp_k)$ is constant in the tetrahedron and points into region $l$. We pick
two orthogonal unit vectors $e_1, e_2$ in that plane with $e_1 \times e_2 = n/\lVert n\rVert$, give each
corner $p$ the angle $\psi = \operatorname{atan2}\big((p-\bar p)\cdot e_2,\,
(p-\bar p)\cdot e_1\big)$ about the centroid $\bar p$ of the corners, sort by $\psi$,
and join the first corner to each consecutive pair. Since the polygon is
convex, its centroid lies inside it and the angular order is the order along
the boundary, and a fan from one corner of a convex polygon covers it exactly
without overlap. Because $n$ always points into the higher label, every
triangle faces the same way.

As an example, take a patch in the plane $z = 0.3$ with $n = (0,0,1)$, so that
$e_1$ and $e_2$ can be taken along $x$ and $y$, and four corners that arrive
in the order $p_1 = (0.2, 0.1)$, $p_2 = (0.6, 0.5)$, $p_3 = (0.6, 0.1)$ and
$p_4 = (0.2, 0.6)$ in the $xy$-coordinates of that plane. Their centroid is
$(0.4, 0.325)$, about which their angles are $-131.6^\circ$, $41.2^\circ$,
$-48.4^\circ$ and $126.0^\circ$. Sorting gives the order $p_1, p_3, p_2, p_4$,
which runs counterclockwise around the polygon, and the fan gives the
triangles $(p_1, p_3, p_2)$ and $(p_1, p_2, p_4)$, with signed areas $0.08$
and $0.10$. Both face along $n$, and together they cover the polygon's area of
$0.18$. Fanning the corners in the order in which they arrived would instead
give $(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ and $(p_1, p_3, p_4)$; the first faces the wrong way,
with signed area $-0.08$, and the two triangles overlap while leaving part of
the polygon uncovered (Figure~\ref{fig:fan}).

\section{Gradients}
\label{sec:gradients-supp}

This section expands Section~\ref{sec:gradients}. It gives the derivative of
a vertex in closed form, states where the extracted mesh is differentiable,
and reports the finite-difference check of Section~\ref{sec:further}.

\subsection{Chain rule}

The extractor is a map from the node logits $L$, the collection of the
vectors $L_v \in \mathbb{R}^K$ over all grid nodes $v$, to the vertex
positions of the output mesh. The node logits are in turn produced by the field, $L_v = f_\theta(v) +
\varepsilon_v$, where $\theta$ denotes its parameters (centers and radii,
power-diagram sites and weights, or the weights of a network) and
$\varepsilon_v$ is the perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}, which is computed from a detached copy of the logits and so is treated as a constant. The grid nodes $v$ are fixed.
For a loss $\mathcal{L}$ evaluated on the mesh, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta}
  \;=\; \sum_{x} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x}\,
        \sum_{v} \frac{\partial x}{\partial L_v}\,
        \frac{\partial f_\theta(v)}{\partial \theta},
  \label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
where $x$ runs over the mesh vertices. This is the chain rule from $\theta$
through the node logits and the vertices to the loss. Each factor is a
Jacobian, of size $1\times3$, $3\times K$ and $K\times P$ for $P$ parameters,
and the inner sum has at most four nonzero terms, since a vertex depends only
on the nodes of its simplex. The first factor depends on the loss and the last
on the field; the middle factor is supplied by the extractor and
is given in closed form below. ``Exact gradients'' means that \eqref{eq:chain}
is, up to floating-point rounding, the derivative of the mesh that the
extractor returns with $\varepsilon_v$ held fixed. With the perturbation off
this is the returned mesh itself. With it on, $\varepsilon_v$ scales with
$\max_{u,j}|L_u[j]|$ and so depends on $\theta$, and the two derivatives
differ by $O(\varepsilon)$. Neither is the derivative of the true interface
of $f_\theta$, from which the returned mesh differs by the interpolation
error.

\subsection{Derivative of a vertex}

Consider a vertex with tied label set $S = \{l_0,\dots,l_{m-1}\}$ on the
$(m-1)$-simplex with nodes $v_0,\dots,v_{m-1}$ and logit vectors
$L_0,\dots,L_{m-1}$ (Section~\ref{sec:solve}). The solve \eqref{eq:solve},
$A(L)\,\lambda' = b(L)$, gives its barycentric coordinates
$\lambda' = (\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{m-1})$, with
$\lambda_0 = 1 - \sum_{a\ge1}\lambda_a$, and its position is
$x = v_0 + E\lambda'$, where the columns of $E \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times(m-1)}$
are the edge vectors $v_a - v_0$. Let
$\rho_r(\lambda, L) = \sum_{a=0}^{m-1} \lambda_a \big(L_a[l_0] - L_a[l_r]\big)$
for $r = 1,\dots,m-1$, the difference $\interp_{l_0} - \interp_{l_r}$ at the
point with barycentric coordinates $\lambda$. The vertex is where every
$\rho_r$ vanishes, and substituting $\lambda_0$ gives $\rho = A\lambda' - b$,
so $\partial\rho/\partial\lambda' = A$. Differentiating $\rho = 0$ at the
vertex with respect to a logit $L_c[k]$ of one of the nodes gives
$A\,\partial\lambda'/\partial L_c[k] + \beta = 0$, where
$\beta = \partial \rho/\partial L_c[k]$ at fixed $\lambda$ has the components
\begin{equation}
  \beta_r \;=\; \frac{\partial \rho_r}{\partial L_c[k]}
       \;=\; \begin{cases} \lambda_c & k = l_0,\\ -\lambda_c & k = l_r,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
  \label{eq:w}
\end{equation}
Hence the middle factor $\partial x/\partial L_{v_c}$ of \eqref{eq:chain} has
the columns
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial x}{\partial L_c[k]}
  \;=\; E\,\frac{\partial \lambda'}{\partial L_c[k]}
  \;=\; -\,E A^{-1} \beta .
  \label{eq:jac}
\end{equation}
The derivative is nonzero only for the
nodes of the vertex's own simplex and only for the labels in $S$, so each
vertex depends on at most $m^2$ of the node logits. A node $v_c$ contributes
in proportion to its barycentric weight $\lambda_c$, so a vertex close to a
node is sensitive mostly to that node. The derivative is a rational
function of $L$, finite wherever $A$ is invertible. For a crossing, $m=2$,
$x = v_0 + t\,(v_1 - v_0)$ with $t = \lambda_1 = d_0/(d_0 - d_1)$ and
$d_a = L_a[l_0] - L_a[l_1]$, and \eqref{eq:jac} becomes
$\partial x/\partial L_a[l_0] = -\,\partial x/\partial L_a[l_1]
= (v_1 - v_0)\,\partial t/\partial d_a$ with
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial t}{\partial d_0} = \frac{-d_1}{(d_0-d_1)^2}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial t}{\partial d_1} = \frac{d_0}{(d_0-d_1)^2}.
  \label{eq:jac2}
\end{equation}
The implementation does not evaluate \eqref{eq:jac}; autograd differentiates
the solve directly. On $200$ random simplices for each of $m = 2, 3, 4$,
autograd through a direct implementation of \eqref{eq:solve} agrees with
\eqref{eq:jac} to $10^{-15}$ relative error.

\subsection{Changes of structure}

The combinatorial structure of the output, namely which tied sets yield
vertices and how the vertices are connected, is decided by inequalities on
$L$: that the solution of \eqref{eq:solve} lies in its simplex
($\lambda_a \ge -\delta$) and that no label outside $S$ exceeds the tied
labels there by more than $\delta$, with the tolerance $\delta$ of
Section~\ref{sec:predicates}. These decisions are taken on a detached copy of $L$, so they carry no
gradient. On any open set of node logits on which all of them keep their
outcome and every $A$ is invertible, the structure is constant and every
vertex position is a smooth function of $L$ with derivative \eqref{eq:jac};
hence so
is any loss that is a smooth function of the vertex positions, such as an
area, a volume or a length. On the boundary of such a set a vertex is created
or removed, and the derivative of the loss need not exist there.
Section~\ref{sec:contact} gives the example of contact onset,
where a patch area is continuous but its derivative jumps from $-\pi r$ to
$0$. The perturbation makes the invertibility of $A$ generic but does not
remove these boundaries, which are a property of the quantity being
differentiated.

\subsection{Finite-difference check}

To confirm that autograd returns the derivatives of what the extractor
computes, we compare it against central finite differences, with step
$10^{-6}$, of the loss $\sum_v (\langle w, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 +
v_1 v_2 v_3)$ over the extracted vertices $v$, for a fixed $w$. A sum over
the vertices does not depend on their order, which a change of parameter can
permute without moving the surface. Each probe moves one parameter both
ways; probes at which the vertex count changes are changes of structure,
where a difference quotient is undefined, and are skipped and counted. The
error of a probe is the gap between autograd and the finite difference $d$,
divided by $\max(1, |d|)$.
At resolution $12$ the worst is $1.1\times10^{-8}$ for a power diagram
differentiated with respect to all sites and weights ($20$ probes),
$2.6\times10^{-8}$ for shells with respect to radii and $3.7\times10^{-10}$ for a
sector fan with respect to its center. A neural field smaller than those of
Section~\ref{sec:fields-supp} ($K=4$, width $32$, $j \le 2$, seed $1$) gives
$7.9\times10^{-8}$ at resolution $10$ for $81$ of its $2\,948$ weights,
spread evenly over every layer and bias ($81$ probes, none skipped).

\section{Proofs}
\label{sec:proofs-supp}

The propositions are stated in Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}. All of the proofs
use two properties of the $P_1$ interpolant: on each tetrahedron every
$\interp^\tet_k$ is affine, and on a face or an edge $\interp$ depends only on
the logits at its nodes. The results about a single cell follow from these and
genericity alone. Conformity, completeness and differentiability also use the
construction: shared edges and faces, the growth of the candidate sets, and
closed-form solves decided on detached logits. Exactness on affine logits
uses the interpolant and the construction. Each proof uses only results
proved before it.

\subsection{Properties of a single cell}
\label{sec:proofs-cell}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:convex} (Convexity).}
\begin{proof}
$\interp^\tet_k - \interp^\tet_j$ is affine, so
$\{\interp^\tet_k \ge \interp^\tet_j\}$ is a closed half-space, all of space
or empty, and $\dregion{k}\cap\tet$, the intersection of $\tet$ with $K-1$
such sets, is a convex polyhedron. The patch
$\dregion{k}\cap\dregion{l}\cap\tet$ is the set where
$\interp_k = \interp_l \ge \interp_j$ for every $j$, that is, this polyhedron
cut by the set $\{\interp_k=\interp_l\}$. On a generic field
$\interp_k - \interp_l$ is not identically zero on $\tet$, so that set is a
plane or empty and the patch is a convex polygon; this is where genericity is
used, since were the two labels equal throughout $\tet$, the patch would be
the whole of $\dregion{k}\cap\tet$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Corollary~\ref{cor:fan}.}
\begin{proof}
The corners of a patch are the vertices of the convex polygon of
Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}, so the patch is their convex hull, and the
triangulation is that of Section~\ref{sec:assembly-supp}. It remains to
check that on a generic field a non-empty patch is two-dimensional with
distinct corners in strictly convex position. A patch that is a segment or a
point is cut down to it either by $\tet$, when the plane
$\{\interp_k=\interp_l\}$ meets $\tet$ only along an edge or at a node, so
that its vertices lie on the boundary of their simplex, or by a third label
that ties with $k$ and $l$ along the whole of it, which is a degenerate tie.
Two coinciding corners carry either different tied sets, so that at one of
them a label outside its tied set equals it, or the same tied set on two
different simplices, so that the point lies on the boundary of both. Three
collinear corners make two of the lines bounding the polygon coincide, which
puts a triple curve in a facet of $\tet$, four labels in a tie along a line,
or an edge of $\tet$ in the plane of the patch. Genericity excludes each of
these.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} (Vertex classification).}
\begin{proof}
Let the labels of a set $S$ with $|S| = m$ tie at the vertex, and fix
$k \in S$. A fifth label tying there would be exactly equal to the tied set
of the other four, which genericity excludes, so $m \le 4$. The vertex is a
corner of the convex polyhedron $\dregion{k}\cap\tet$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}), so the constraints active at it have rank
three. On a generic field these are the $m-1$ equalities
$\interp_k = \interp_j$, $j \in S\setminus\{k\}$, and the facet planes of
$\tet$ through the vertex; no other label equals the tied set there. The
$m-1$ equalities are independent, since the system \eqref{eq:solve} of the
tie is nonsingular. This is where genericity is used. For the degenerate
field $f_1 \equiv 0$, $f_2 = x_1$, $f_3 = 2x_1$ all three labels tie on the
whole plane $x_1 = 0$, and $m = 3$ supplies one constraint instead of two.
The facet planes must supply the remaining $4-m$ independent constraints, so
the vertex lies on at least $4-m$ facets of $\tet$, whose common face has
dimension at most $m-1$. On a generic field the vertex does not lie on the
boundary of that face, so the dimension is exactly $m-1$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:plateau} (Force balance at triple curves).}
\begin{proof}
The patch separating $k$ and $l$ lies in the level set of
$\interp_l - \interp_k$, whose gradient is $g_l - g_k = n_{kl}$. The sum
$n_{kl} + n_{lp} + n_{pk} = (g_l - g_k) + (g_p - g_l) + (g_k - g_p)$
telescopes to zero. The curve lies in both the $k|l$ and the $l|p$ patch, so
its direction is orthogonal to $n_{kl}$ and $n_{lp}$, and by the vanishing sum
also to $n_{pk}$.

For the angles, place the origin at a point of the curve, so that the three
interpolated logits share their value there and their constant terms may be
dropped, and work in the plane $\Pi$ orthogonal to the curve. Let $a_k$ be the projection of
$g_k$ onto $\Pi$, so that $n_{kl} = a_l - a_k$. Near the curve the three
labels are the only contenders, so a neighborhood of the origin in $\Pi$ is
partitioned into the argmax cones of the linear maps $x \mapsto a_k\!\cdot\!x$.
The cone occupied by label $l$ is
$\{x : (a_l - a_k)\!\cdot\!x \ge 0,\ (a_l - a_p)\!\cdot\!x \ge 0\}$, which is
the normal cone of the triangle $a_k a_l a_p$ at its vertex $a_l$. A cone
bounded by two half-planes with inward normals $q$ and $q'$ has opening angle
$180^\circ$ minus the angle between $q$ and $q'$, so the dihedral angle of
region $l$ is $180^\circ$ minus the interior angle of the triangle at $a_l$,
and the three dihedral angles sum to $3\cdot180^\circ - 180^\circ = 360^\circ$.
The triangle is non-degenerate because $n_{kl}$ and $n_{lp}$ are
independent, which is the condition for three patches rather than two to meet
along the curve. All three dihedral angles equal $120^\circ$ if and only if
all three interior angles equal $60^\circ$, that is, if and only if the
triangle is equilateral, which means that its side lengths
$\|a_l - a_k\| = \|n_{kl}\|$, $\|n_{lp}\|$ and $\|n_{pk}\|$ are equal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Properties of the construction}
\label{sec:proofs-construction}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:conform} (Conformity).}
\begin{proof}
$\interp^\tet|_\varphi$ and $\interp^{\tet'}|_\varphi$ are both the $P_1$
interpolant of the same three node logit vectors, so they are one function;
a crossing on an edge of $\varphi$ is determined by \eqref{eq:solve} from the
two node logit vectors of that edge, and a triple point in $\varphi$ from its
three. In the implementation the agreement is exact rather than up to
rounding, because edges and faces are not enumerated per tetrahedron but
deduplicated into global entities keyed by their sorted node indices. Each
face is a single entity with one ordering of its nodes, its vertices are
solved once from that ordering, and $\tet$ and $\tet'$ refer to the result,
position and acceptance decision alike, by the same index.

A four-label tie is solved per tetrahedron, from four node logit vectors, so
$\tet$ and $\tet'$ would solve different systems for one. On a generic field
such a tie lies strictly inside its tetrahedron and never on $\varphi$, which
is why genericity is assumed.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} (Completeness of the candidate sets).}
\begin{proof}
Edges come first. Every label pair is tested on an edge whose endpoints
differ, and an edge whose endpoints carry the same label carries no other,
since each region meets an edge in a single interval
(Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}). Every label whose region meets an edge is
therefore found on it.

Now let $\sigma$ be a face or a tetrahedron, let $S$ be its candidate set when
the growth stops, and suppose that the region of a label $q \notin S$ meets
$\sigma$. The set $S$ contains every label whose region meets the boundary of
$\sigma$, for a face by the case of edges and for a tetrahedron by the case
of faces, since the set of a tetrahedron is seeded from the final sets of its
faces. On the affine span of $\sigma$ consider
\[
  h(x) \;=\; \interp_q(x) - \max_{j \in S} \interp_j(x)
       \;=\; \min_{j \in S} \big(\interp_q(x) - \interp_j(x)\big),
\]
a minimum of affine functions, hence concave and piecewise affine. On the
boundary of $\sigma$ the largest label lies in $S$, so $h \le 0$ there. On a
generic field $q$ is strictly the largest label at some point of the relative
interior of $\sigma$, so $h > 0$ there. The set $\{h > 0\}$ is convex, meets
the interior of $\sigma$ and not its boundary, and therefore lies inside
$\sigma$. The maximum of $h$ is thus attained, and the set of maximizers is
convex and bounded. Let $x^\ast$ be one of its extreme points, and call a
piece $\interp_q - \interp_j$ active if it equals $h$ at $x^\ast$. The
gradients of the active pieces, taken within the affine span of $\sigma$,
contain $0$ in their convex hull, since $x^\ast$ maximizes the concave
function $h$, and span every direction of that span, since otherwise $h$
would be constant on a segment through $x^\ast$ and $x^\ast$ would not be an
extreme point. In $d = \dim\sigma$ dimensions this takes at least $d+1$
gradients, since $d$ spanning vectors are independent and $0$ is not a convex
combination of independent vectors. At $x^\ast$, then, at least $d+1$ labels
of $S$ tie as the largest of $S$, and on a generic field exactly $d+1$. The
enumeration tries this label set, finds its tie at $x^\ast$ inside
$\sigma$, and finds it beaten, since
$\interp_q(x^\ast) > \max_{j\in S}\interp_j(x^\ast)$. The label that is
largest at $x^\ast$ is therefore not in $S$, and the growth adds it, which
contradicts the assumption that the growth has stopped.

In floating point the tie at $x^\ast$ counts as beaten only by a margin
above $\delta$. Since $h \ge \interp_q - \max_{j \ne q}\interp_j$, this holds
whenever $q$ exceeds every other label by more than $\delta$ somewhere in
$\sigma$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:diff} (Differentiability).}
\begin{proof}
The predicates deciding the structure are the argmax at each node, which gates
which edges are examined at all, containment of each candidate tie in its
simplex, and dominance at each tie. By hypothesis every one of them holds
strictly at $\theta_0$, and each is a strict inequality between functions of
$\theta$ that are continuous near $\theta_0$, the tie positions because
$\det A \neq 0$ for every enumerated label set, so all of them are locally
constant. Strictness is
needed for the rejected candidates as much as for the accepted ones, since a
candidate beaten by exactly zero margin is admitted by the extractor but
eliminated by an arbitrarily small perturbation, which changes the structure.
With the
structure fixed, a vertex is $\sum_a \lambda_a v_a$ with
$\lambda' = A(L)^{-1}b(L)$, a rational function of $L$ with non-vanishing
denominator, composed with the differentiable map
$\theta \mapsto L = f_\theta(\text{nodes})$. Reverse-mode accumulation over
that composition realizes its Jacobian because the combinatorial decisions are
taken on detached logits and contribute no edges to the graph; with the
perturbation on, this is the Jacobian with its displacement held fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:gradients-supp}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exactness on affine logits}
\label{sec:proofs-affine}

\paragraph*{Proposition~\ref{prop:affine} (Exactness on affine logits).}
\begin{proof}
The $P_1$ interpolant is linear in the node values and reproduces affine
functions, so $\interp_k - \interp_l$, the interpolant of the affine function
$f_k - f_l$, equals it. The argmax of $\interp$ is therefore that of $f$,
\eqref{eq:dregion} defines the closures of the regions $\region{k}$, and the
object to be extracted is the exact partition of $f$. On a generic field,
which is where genericity is used, every vertex of the partition is a tie of
$m \le 4$ labels on a face of dimension $m-1$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}), the candidate set of that face holds all
$m$ labels (Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}), so the tie is enumerated, and
it is kept, since it lies in its simplex and no label beats it there; no
other tie is kept, since dominance is tested against all $K$ labels.
Corollary~\ref{cor:fan} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:conform} then assemble these vertices into the
complex. For the second claim, the power cell of site $c_k$ with weight $w_k$
is defined by minimizing $\|x-c_k\|^2 - w_k$~\cite{aurenhammer1987}, that is,
by maximizing the logit $-\|x-c_k\|^2 + w_k$ of
Section~\ref{sec:fields-supp}, and the difference of two such logits,
$2(c_k - c_l)\!\cdot\!x - \|c_k\|^2 + \|c_l\|^2 + w_k - w_l$, is affine.
\end{proof}

\label{sec:degentest-supp}

Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} resolves exact coincidences by displacing the
logits rather than deciding them exactly, the weaker of the two guarantees
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). This section measures the error that the displacement introduces.

The test uses the degeneracy that has no analog with two labels. In 3D four
regions meet at a point generically, five only by coincidence, for example
when five sites lie on a common sphere, whose center is then equidistant from
all five. Pushing one site outward by $t$ splits this point into two
quadruple points about $t$ apart, so letting $t$ shrink drives the
configuration toward the degeneracy. For each $t$ the exact
quadruple points are computed by an independent enumeration.
Figure~\ref{fig:degensetup} shows the configuration, and
Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy} reports the largest distance from a true quadruple

Close to a degeneracy, the perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}, of

$\varepsilon/t$. The measured error is instead at most $1.79\varepsilon$ and
does not grow as $t$ shrinks, even when the two points are only $10^{-10}$
apart, and it is the same multiple of $\varepsilon$ for all three sizes until
$\varepsilon$ is ten times their separation. The error stays bounded because
each quadruple point solves its own $3\times3$ system, the ties among its
four labels, which the fifth label does not enter. With the perturbation switched off the
error is at most $1.1\times10^{-16}$ throughout.

The ratio changes only in the last column, from $0.85$ to $0.51$ and $0.46$
at $t \le 10^{-9}$, where $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ is ten to a hundred times the
separation of the two points. The positions are still accurate to
$O(\varepsilon)$ there, but the relative arrangement of the two points is no
longer resolved. The perturbation therefore has to stay below the smallest
feature of the geometry.

It must also be large enough. With $s$ the logit scale and $\delta = 10^{-12}$
the tolerance of Section~\ref{sec:predicates}, two labels tied at a node move at least
$\varepsilon s/K$ apart, so the crossings there lie more than $\delta$ inside
their edges once $\varepsilon > 2K\delta$ ($3.2\times10^{-11}$ at $K = 16$).
The two-label field $f(x) = (x_1, 0)$, whose labels tie on the whole grid
plane $x_1 = 0$, gives a closed mesh at resolution $48$, one that passes the
check of Section~\ref{sec:closure-supp}, for
every $\varepsilon$ from $10^{-11}$ to $10^{-7}$. Ties of three labels are not
covered. On the three-label field $f(x) = (x_1, 0, x_2)$, whose labels all tie
on the grid line $x_1 = x_2 = 0$, at $637$
nodes over the $13$ even resolutions from $36$ to $60$, $13$, $10$, $2$ and $1$ meshes
are open, that is, fail that check, at $\varepsilon = 10^{-11}$, $10^{-10}$, $10^{-9}$ and $10^{-8}$, all
holes within a grid step of the line.

predicates of Du et al.~\cite{du2022} guarantee correct combinatorial

\section{Vertex distance to the interface}
\label{sec:pointwise-supp}

Volume and area are integrals over the whole surface, so they can show that
a surface is wrong but not where. A local quantity is
the distance from each vertex to the interface it should lie on. On a power
diagram this has a closed form. Each difference $f_i - f_j$ is affine, so if
$f_i$ and $f_j$ are the two largest logits at a vertex, dividing $f_i - f_j$
there by $\lVert\nabla(f_i-f_j)\rVert = 2\lVert c_i - c_j\rVert$ gives the
exact distance from the vertex to the plane where the two are equal. The
distance is zero exactly when the vertex lies on an interface.

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy} applies this measure to the enclosed cell of
Table~\ref{tab:exact} and Figure~\ref{fig:failure}, a truncated cube enclosed
by $14$ other cells. The polyhedron is extracted from the same field on the
same grid in three ways: by our method, by corner-label case analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:failure}), and by \texttt{vtkSurfaceNets3D}~\cite{vtk}.
SurfaceNets is the multi-label extractor of VTK, with one pass
over the labels, points and cells shared between adjacent regions and no gaps
to repair, but it takes a label map, so it is given the argmax of the field
at the grid nodes. The top row of the figure shows the three surfaces at
resolution $16$. A face is gray if its vertices lie on the interface to
machine precision, and otherwise colored by the distance of its farthest
vertex, from yellow near zero to dark red at half a grid cell or more. The
bottom row gives the worst and median distance against resolution, and the
distance of every vertex at resolution $48$, the resolution of the numbers
below.

Our method places each of its $8\,819$ vertices on the interface to machine
precision, so its surface is gray throughout, and it recovers each of the
$24$ corners of the polyhedron to within $10^{-14}$. The corner-label method
places most vertices exactly, but $2.0\%$ are off the interface, the worst by
$8.0\times10^{-2}$, which is $1.93$ grid cells. Nearly all of them lie within
a grid cell of an edge of the polyhedron, that is, of a triple curve, where
the labels at the corners of a grid cell do not determine its patch; in the
top row they form the colored slivers along the edges. This worst case does
not converge, moving only from $9.3\times10^{-2}$ at resolution $8$ to
$8.0\times10^{-2}$ at $48$, because the number of grid faces that a triple
curve passes near grows as fast as the number it crosses
(Section~\ref{sec:failure}), so refinement does not thin them out.

Every SurfaceNets vertex is off the interface, by $3.3\times10^{-3}$ at the
median and $1.6\times10^{-2}$ at worst, and the error converges at roughly
first order. The error comes from its input, since a label map records only
which label wins at each node, and the logits that locate the interface
between nodes cannot be recovered from it. Its
surface is therefore off everywhere by a fraction of a grid cell, so that
every face in the top row is colored, and the edges and corners of the
polyhedron are rounded. We include the
comparison because label maps are what segmentation pipelines usually pass to
a surface extractor, and Section~\ref{sec:realdata} shows the same effect on
the output of a trained network.

\subsection{Convergence on curved interfaces}
\label{sec:curved-supp}

On a curved interface exactness is impossible, and the relevant question is
the order at which the error decreases under refinement. On the spherical
shells the extracted area of the innermost interface, the sphere of radius
$0.30$, has relative error
$4.56\times10^{-2}$, $2.01\times10^{-2}$, $1.12\times10^{-2}$ and
$5.00\times10^{-3}$ at resolutions $16, 24, 32, 48$, which corresponds to a
least-squares fitted rate of $2.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}, right).
Second order is the order that a $P_1$ interpolant of the logits supports,
and it is also the order of marching cubes on a smooth two-label field. The
shells have no junctions;
convergence at curved junctions is measured on the double bubble
(Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}).

\subsection{Tied logits at emitted vertices}
\label{sec:consistency-supp}

Every emitted vertex is defined as a point at which a given set of logits
tie (Equation~\eqref{eq:solve}). This check tests
whether the emitted position satisfies that definition independently of the
implementation of the solve. The interpolant is recomputed
at each emitted vertex by locating it in the grid and evaluating
\eqref{eq:interp}, and the spread among the logits that are supposed to tie,
the largest minus the smallest, is measured. Over five fields at resolution $14$, the worst spread is $4.4\times10^{-16}$
(power diagram, $2\,499$ vertices), $2.2\times10^{-16}$ (sector fan, $2\,106$),
$5.6\times10^{-17}$ (shells, $3\,402$), $2.1\times10^{-15}$ (neural $K=4$,
$8\,460$) and $2.8\times10^{-15}$ (neural $K=6$, $11\,418$).

\subsection{Closure and orientation}
\label{sec:closure-supp}

Each extracted region should be a closed surface with a consistent
orientation. We check this one triangle side at a time. Every side of every
triangle is examined together with the label pair of its patch. A side may be used only
once if it lies on a triple curve, where three patches meet along a
non-manifold edge, or on $\partial\Omega$, where the surface is legitimately
cut off; every other side must be used exactly twice and traversed in
opposite directions by its two users. A mesh that passes this check is what
we call closed, or watertight. Across five fields at resolutions $12$
and $20$, with up to $45\,446$ triangles and $74\,901$ sides, there are no
unpaired sides, no inconsistently oriented sides and no patches with too few
corners.

\paragraph*{Optimization.} One scalar grid of $33^3$ values, initialized to
the signed distance of a sphere, drives both extractors under the same
objective. At each step $2\,048$ points are drawn uniformly by area from the
extracted mesh, and the objective is their symmetric Chamfer distance to a
fixed target cloud of $4\,096$ points: the mean squared distance from each
point to the nearest point of the other set, summed over both directions.
The targets are an analytic ellipsoid, which
is a pure deformation of the start, and an analytic torus, which additionally
requires a change of genus, that is, a hole opened through the surface;
accuracy is scored against the analytic surface
rather than against the point cloud. Only the grid is optimized, so the two
gradient paths are compared with the rest of each method fixed. FlexiCubes
also carries per-cube weights, and freezing them would test only its
gradient path, so a third run frees them and adds the regularizer that
Shen et al.~\cite{shen2023} use with free weights, which penalizes the spread
of the distances from each dual vertex to the crossings of its cell. Each run
is swept over the learning rate and over the weight of a smoothness penalty,
the mean squared seven-point discrete Laplacian of the grid values, and the
setting with the lowest objective, averaged over the last $20$ of $250$
steps, is reported, because the two methods respond differently to the same
setting. Our trace oscillates at the largest learning rate while that of
FlexiCubes is flat, and smoothing the grid helps our method more than it
helps FlexiCubes.

Our objective settles $29\%$ above those of the two FlexiCubes runs. On
the true surface the difference is larger, and the FlexiCubes surface lies
about six times closer to the ellipsoid than ours (Table~\ref{tab:fcopt}).
Part of our error comes
from small separate pieces of surface that appear away from the target
during the descent; without them it is $0.17$ cells, still four times that
of FlexiCubes. The remaining error is consistent with noise in the field,
which our vertices reproduce and FlexiCubes damps by placing each vertex at
an average of the crossings of its cell; at $K=2$ this is an advantage of
FlexiCubes. Neither method achieves the change of genus that
the torus requires from this initialization.

The smoothness penalty isolates the difference, because it acts on
the shared grid and is therefore the same change for both methods. At the
selected learning rate of each method it reduces our error from
$0.469$ to $0.274$ cells, while that of FlexiCubes moves only from $0.049$ to
$0.043$. The penalty thus removes almost half of our excess error and little
of that of FlexiCubes, but it does not close the gap. Stronger smoothing
does not help further; at ten times the selected weight our error is $1.44$
cells, worse than with no smoothing at all.

setting wins the sweep. The table quotes the released run log; no
comparison here depends on differences of that size.

\paragraph*{Three regions by one-versus-rest.} This test asks whether a
single-field extractor can return a partition of three regions. The regions
are those of the standard double bubble (Figure~\ref{fig:fcsetup}, bottom),
whose true partition is known in closed form: the exterior and two equal
lobes, which meet along a flat wall and a circular triple curve. A
single-field extractor takes one region at a time,
so each lobe $k$ is extracted on its own, as the zero set of
$\max_{j\neq k} f_j - f_k$, which is negative inside the lobe and positive
outside it; the exterior is what neither lobe claims. Our extractor is run
once on all three labels and returns the three regions as
one complex (rows ``ours, one complex'' of Table~\ref{tab:fcjunction}).
Whether the two lobes
and the exterior cover each point of the domain exactly once is measured on
$40\,000$ points drawn uniformly from it, each found inside or outside each
extracted surface by its generalized winding number~\cite{jacobson2013}, the
solid angle that the surface subtends at the point divided by $4\pi$, which
is $1$ inside a closed surface and $0$ outside; a point is inside when its
absolute value exceeds one half. A point inside both lobes counts as overlap,
a point of a true lobe inside neither counts as a gap, and a point whose
label differs from the true one counts as an error, a point inside both
lobes being given to the first.

FlexiCubes does not return a partition at any resolution. From resolution $16$ to $48$ its overlap grows
from $0.90\%$ to $1.41\%$ while its gap falls from $2.91\%$ to $1.87\%$, so
refinement trades one against the other, and its error falls only from
$3.90\%$ to $3.29\%$.

The failure could come from asking for one region at a time or from the way
FlexiCubes places its vertices. The field $\max_{j\neq k} f_j - f_k$ has a
crease along the triple curve, where the maximum passes from one label to
another, and FlexiCubes places each vertex at an average of the crossings of
its cell, which rounds off a crease. A control separates the two. With our
extractor in place of FlexiCubes, given the same two fields and scored in the
same way (rows ``ours, one-versus-rest''), the overlap is zero at every
resolution and the error falls from $0.67\%$ to $0.08\%$, close to that of
our one-complex extraction. The one-versus-rest split therefore accounts for
little of the error; most of the three percent comes from where FlexiCubes
places its vertices near the crease.

Our two one-versus-rest extractions cannot overlap, except within the $O(\varepsilon)$ distance by which
the perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} moves each surface. Write $I(g)$ for the $P_1$ interpolant of a function $g$. Each extraction
returns the exact zero set of the interpolant of its field, so lobe $k$
claims the points where $I(f_k) > I(\max_{j\neq k} f_j)$. The interpolant
combines node values with nonnegative weights, so
$I(\max_{j\neq k} f_j) \ge I(f_j)$ for every $j \neq k$, and a point claimed
by both lobes would need $I(f_1) > I(f_2)$ and $I(f_2) > I(f_1)$ at once. In
cells where both lobes exceed the exterior at every node, the two fields
interpolate to $\pm(I(f_1) - I(f_2))$, so both extractions put the wall in
the same place; only near the triple curve, where interpolating the maximum
and taking the maximum of the interpolants differ, do they leave a thin gap.
A FlexiCubes vertex has no such property. Near the crease the two
extractions average different crossings, and the overlap they leave does not
shrink under refinement.

Our one-complex extraction has the lowest error of the three at every
resolution, falling from $0.51\%$ to $0.06\%$. It emits the shared wall once
and contains the triple curve as an edge, which the contact operator of
Section~\ref{sec:contact} requires.

\section{Double-bubble ablation}
\label{sec:ablation-supp}

Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble} replaces our extractor by the corner-label
extractor in the double-bubble optimization and attributes the failure to
the case analysis. The baseline reads the features off the corner labels,
locates them by bisection and Newton iteration instead of the closed-form
solve of Section~\ref{sec:solve}, iterates on the field itself rather than
on the $P_1$ interpolant, and has no perturbation. Any of these differences
could change which features are emitted or make the objective trace noisy.

Two more extractors, the second and third rows of Table~\ref{tab:ablation},
separate these differences. Both read a cell as the case table does, with one
crossing on each edge whose end labels differ, between those two labels, and
one tie on each face or tetrahedron whose corner labels are all distinct,
with no other label set tried and no dominance test. They then locate these
features with our closed-form solves on the interpolant and assemble them as
our method does, one with the perturbation and one without. Each is
therefore our method with the enumeration replaced by the case table and
no other change. On five-site power diagrams at three seeds, all three
corner-label extractors emit the same vertex and triangle counts and differ
only in where the vertices lie, because the baseline clamps its Newton
iterate to an enlarged cell and the closed form does not.

The case analysis alone changes the outcome. With the solver, interpolant,
objective, schedule and seed unchanged, and only the proposed features
different, the ratio $r_1/d$ of the first radius to the distance between the
centers moves from $1.0064$ to $0.7646$, the dihedral angles
spread from $119.8^\circ$--$120.3^\circ$ to $97.9^\circ$--$131.1^\circ$, and
the mesh is no longer watertight. Without the perturbation the ratio is
$0.9423$, again not watertight. The failure is therefore caused neither by
the baseline's solver nor by its lack of a perturbation.

The noise in the objective trace has a different cause. With $a_t$ the value
of $A/\bar V^{2/3}$ at step $t$, we score a trace by the median of
$|a_{t+1} - 2a_t + a_{t-1}|$ over the median of $|a_{t+1} - a_t|$, both over
the second half of the run, that is, by how much it reverses relative to how
fast it moves; the second difference alone would measure convergence, since
a converged trace is flat. On this measure our method scores
$0.022$, the corner-label extractors $0.018$ with the perturbation and
$0.023$ without, and the baseline $1.437$. The baseline is the one that
reverses from step to step, so the noise in Figure~\ref{fig:doublebubble}
comes from its iterative solver and the wrong shape from the case analysis.

The three corner-label extractors do not err in the same direction: their
ratios are $0.76$ and $0.94$, below the correct value, and $1.28$, above it.
Their errors therefore do not measure one bias, and the gap cannot be split
into a part due to the case analysis and a part due to the solver. A single
start also says little about where the optimization can end. Over five
starts (Table~\ref{tab:basins}), with $(r_1/d, r_2/d)$ equal to
$(0.75, 0.57)$ as in Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}, $(0.87, 0.87)$,
$(0.78, 0.47)$, $(0.92, 0.96)$ and $(0.67, 0.64)$, the corner-label extractors end between
$0.74$ and $1.03$, $0.70$ and $0.97$, and $0.90$ and $2.87$, and none of the
fifteen runs is watertight. Our method returns $1.006$ to $1.009$ from four
of the five starts and is watertight from all five. The value
$r_1/d = 1.276$ is thus one sample from a wide range of end points away from
the double bubble, as the refinement sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble} also finds by varying the grid.

Our method fails from the most lopsided start, $(0.78, 0.47)$. It ends at
$r_1/d = 0.4592$ with $A/\bar V^{2/3} = 9.6832$, the value $9.6720$ of two
separate balls up to the discretization error. The lobes came apart, and
with no shared wall there is no triple curve and no gradient to pull them
back together. The output is still watertight and correctly represents a
field whose lobes have separated, so this is a failure of the descent rather
than of the extractor, but it is a dependence on initialization, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} records it.

\section{Contact between solid objects}
\label{sec:contact-supp}

Sections~\ref{sec:demos} and~\ref{sec:contact} summarize the accuracy of
the contact set; this section gives the closed forms, the full table and the
setup of the optimization. The field is that of Section~\ref{sec:demos},
with $f_0 \equiv 0$ and $f_k(x) = r_k^2 - \lVert x - c_k\rVert^2$ for ball $k$.

\paragraph*{Two balls.} Take two balls of radius $r$ with centers at
$(\pm s/2, 0, 0)$. On the plane $x=0$ both ball logits equal
$r^2 - s^2/4 - y^2 - z^2$, so the wall between them is a disk of radius
$a=\sqrt{r^2-s^2/4}$, and
\[
  A(s) = \pi\!\left(r^2 - \tfrac{s^2}{4}\right),\quad
  \frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}s} = -\frac{\pi s}{2},\quad
  C(s) = 2\pi\sqrt{r^2 - \tfrac{s^2}{4}},
\]
for $s<2r$, with all three zero beyond. Table~\ref{tab:contact} sweeps $s$
across this range. The errors depend on $a/h$, the radius of the disk in
grid cells, and not on the separation. Inside the disk the surface is exact,
because $f_i-f_j$ is affine and so equals its own $P_1$ interpolant. All of
the error is therefore in the position of the rim, and a disk only a few
cells across places its rim coarsely. Automatic differentiation gives the
derivative of the area with respect to the two centers, and the chain rule
turns it into $\mathrm{d}A/\mathrm{d}s$. Agreement with the closed form
tests the geometry. At $s=0.70$ automatic differentiation agrees with a
central difference to a relative error of $1.8\times10^{-10}$ for the area
and $1.3\times10^{-10}$ for the rim length.

\paragraph*{Three balls.} Three balls of radius $r$ have their centers on a
circle of radius $R$. The three ball logits are equal on the axis of that
circle, since every point of the axis is equally far from the three centers.
The background joins them where these logits are also zero, at distance $r$
from each center, which gives two quadruple points at heights
$\pm\sqrt{r^2-R^2}$ above and below the plane of the centers. The segment of
the axis between them is the curve along which the three contact patches
meet. With $r = 0.50$ and $R = 0.35$, the exact heights are $\pm0.357071$ and
the exact length of the curve is $0.714143$. At resolution $48$ the extractor
returns exactly two quadruple points, at $-0.355679$ and $+0.355835$, and a
curve of length $0.711514$. The three patches have equal areas by symmetry,
and the extracted areas differ by at most $8.5\times10^{-4}$ of the largest,
although the grid shares none of that symmetry. Of the $39\,946$ triangles,
$6\,236$ lie between two objects. Each of these is stored once and belongs
to both objects, so there are no duplicate triangles to match up, as there
would be with one mesh per object.

\paragraph*{Optimization.} The optimization of Section~\ref{sec:contact}
starts from this configuration at resolution $40$, where the three contact
areas are equal by symmetry, about $0.380$. The targets are the contact areas
of a second configuration, measured at the same resolution: radii $0.52$,
$0.46$ and $0.55$, and centers on a circle of radius $0.33$, turned by
$0.21$\,rad about its axis. They are $A^\star_{12} = 0.3242$,
$A^\star_{13} = 0.5243$ and $A^\star_{23} = 0.3848$. Since the targets come
from the same family of fields on the same grid, they can be reached; any
remaining miss is the optimizer's. The parameters are the three centers
and the three radii. Adam runs for $140$ steps at learning rate
$8\times10^{-3}$, decayed by a cosine schedule to $5\%$ of that value, and
the loss falls from $0.105$ to $6.8\times10^{-9}$.

\section{Clinical segmentation data}
\label{sec:realdata-supp}

Our vertices are computed from the $P_1$ interpolant of the probabilities,
so their distance to its interface is rounding error and only confirms the
computation. To compare the two methods,
Section~\ref{sec:realdata} measures both against the trilinear interpolant
of the same node values, which neither method uses. In each cube it blends
the eight corner values with weights that are products of one linear weight
per axis: at the point $(t_x,t_y,t_z)$ of a unit cube, the corner $(1,1,0)$
has weight $t_x t_y (1-t_z)$. The $P_1$ interpolant blends only the four
corners of the tetrahedron containing the point, with affine weights. The two
agree at the nodes and along the cube edges, but the product terms curve the
trilinear interface inside each cube, while ours is flat in each tetrahedron.
The distance of a vertex is the gap between the two largest trilinear values
there over the length of its gradient. On the walls between segments
(Figure~\ref{fig:realdataacc}) the median
distance is $0.0095$ voxels for our vertices and $0.090$ voxels for those of
SurfaceNets, and the $90$th percentile is $0.074$ and $0.231$ voxels.

Figure~\ref{fig:realdatajunction} shows the junction in the red box of
Figure~\ref{fig:realdata}a. SurfaceNets labels
each output polygon with the pair of segments it separates, as we label
patches, so both panels use the colors of Figure~\ref{fig:realdata}b. In our
output the three patches end on one shared vertex.
SurfaceNets duplicates that vertex to keep the mesh manifold, and its
smoothing pass pulls the copies $1.32$ voxels apart, the largest separation
among the six groups of duplicated points in the window.
