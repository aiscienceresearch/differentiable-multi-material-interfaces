\begin{abstract}
Differentiable isosurface extraction connects implicit geometry, including
neural fields, to gradient-based optimization, but the methods in current use take a scalar
field and separate only two labels. Multi-material geometry, such as a
segmented medical image or a part made of several materials, contains features
with no two-label counterpart. Three regions meet along a curve and four meet
at a point. Robust arrangement algorithms compute the exact argmax partition of
a piecewise-linear multi-label field, including these junctions, but they
encode vertices implicitly and resolve them with exact predicates, so that no
derivative can be propagated through them. We present an extractor that
computes the same partition on a conforming tetrahedral grid from small
closed-form linear solves, one per tied set of labels. Each vertex is therefore
an explicit differentiable function of the nodal values, and reverse-mode
automatic differentiation provides exact gradients. A small numerical perturbation
takes the place of the exact predicates, and we measure its effect
against the reference implementation of the exact construction. We prove
convexity, conformity and exactness on power diagrams, compare our extractor
with FlexiCubes, and show that minimizing
interface area through it recovers the standard double bubble, whereas
corner-label case analysis, the direct multi-label generalization of marching
cubes, converges to a different shape under the same objective. Because the
partition assigns exactly one label to each point, the same operator makes
contact between solid objects differentiable. The shared surface is emitted
once, and a loss evaluated only on it drives prescribed contact areas to their
targets without a penalty term. We also demonstrate the extractor on the
probability volume of a clinical segmentation network.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An implicit representation stores geometry as a function~\cite{park2019,
mescheder2019}, and most downstream machinery consumes a mesh. When the
function is being optimized, the conversion between the two has to carry
gradients, and a line of work from Deep Marching Cubes~\cite{liao2018} through
MeshSDF~\cite{remelli2020}, DMTet~\cite{shen2021} and FlexiCubes~\cite{shen2023}
has turned isosurface extraction into a differentiable operator. These methods
take a scalar field as input and separate its sub-level set from its
super-level set, so that the output is a partition into two labels.

Much of the geometry produced by segmentation and simulation is a
partition into more than two labels. A segmented medical image assigns one of
several tissue labels to each voxel, a composite part is a partition into
materials, and a grain structure is a partition into crystals. With more than
two labels, the output contains two further kinds of feature. Three regions
meet along a curve and four meet at isolated points, and neither configuration
can occur in the partition induced by a single scalar field.

We adopt the partition model introduced early in the multi-material
extraction literature. Per-label values at the grid nodes are interpolated, and
each point receives the label with the largest interpolated value. We call the
values of one label a \emph{channel}. No channel has a surface of its own. The interfaces are where the largest value
changes (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, left), so two regions meet where their two values tie, three meet along a
curve where three tie, and four at points where four tie. Hege et
al.~\cite{hege1997} formulate this model and approximate the resulting
arrangement by subdividing each cell, and the descendants of their
construction are what CGAL implements today~\cite{cgalmesh3}. Du et
al.~\cite{du2022} compute the arrangement exactly for any number of labels by
maintaining a cell complex within each tetrahedron, with vertices encoded
implicitly and resolved by exact predicates. This encoding makes the
construction robust, but it also means that no vertex coordinate is
available as a function of the input, so no derivative can be propagated
through it. The differentiable extractors mentioned above provide
the derivative, but several of them guarantee two-manifold
output~\cite{shen2023, tetweave2025}, which a triple curve violates. Interface
reconstruction for multi-material hydrodynamics, which starts from volume
fractions, meets the same junctions and reports the same difficulty. We review all three
areas in Section~\ref{sec:related}. In this paper we present an extractor that computes the exact arrangement of Du et al.
while exposing each vertex as an explicit differentiable function of the nodal
values. By differentiable we mean that a loss on the mesh can be
back-propagated to the per-label node values and, by the chain rule, to
the parameters that produced them, such as the weights of a neural field, as long as the
combinatorial structure of the mesh does not change
(\suppp{sec:gradients-supp}). Our construction includes the two-label case, and
Section~\ref{sec:flexicubes} compares it with FlexiCubes~\cite{shen2023}.
With two labels it extracts a known field more accurately and optimizes one
less accurately. On a known field with three regions it returns a
partition, while FlexiCubes, asked for one region at a time, does not produce
one at any resolution we tried.

A concrete motivating application is rule-based cardiac fiber generation,
which assigns a myocyte orientation to every point of a ventricular mesh from
the gradients of a few Laplace--Dirichlet solutions~\cite{bayer2012}. Its
extension to the outflow tracts requires each valve to be available as a
separately identified surface~\cite{doste2019}, since the labels on the
interfaces serve as the Dirichlet boundaries~\cite{svmultiphysics}. At
present the route from a segmentation to such a mesh extracts each boundary,
smooths the staircase, merges and tetrahedralizes the pieces and recovers the
region identities from seed points; each step can move the labeling out of
agreement with the geometry, and the chain has no derivative. We use this pipeline as
motivation only and do not run the fiber computation itself.

Our main contribution is an extractor for the exact arrangement of a
multi-label field whose vertices are explicit differentiable functions of the
input. In detail:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A limitation of corner-label methods.} We show that corner-label case analysis, the mechanism of marching cubes
and its multi-material descendants~\cite{lorensen1987, hege1997}, places
the triple point outside its face on $40$ to $50\%$ of the three-label faces of two power diagrams
and that this fraction does not decrease under refinement
(Section~\ref{sec:failure}).
\item \textbf{Our extractor.} We compute the exact arrangement of Du et al.~\cite{du2022} by a different
construction, in which each vertex is a small closed-form linear solve indexed
by the set of tied labels, so that its position is a differentiable function of
the nodal logits (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{Theory and validation.} We prove convexity of the patches, conformity across cells without a
welding step, that no region is missed, even one inside a single cell, and
exactness on affine logits (Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}),
validate the implementation against closed-form ground truth, and show that it
agrees vertex for vertex with the reference implementation of Du et al.\
wherever the field is generic (Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}).
\item \textbf{Applications to shape optimization.} We recover the standard double bubble by minimizing interface area through
the extractor, where corner-label case analysis under the same objective does
not (Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}), and make the contact set between solid
objects differentiable, so that prescribed contact areas are reached without a
penalty term or a collision query (Section~\ref{sec:contact}).
\item \textbf{Medical data.} We demonstrate the extractor on the probability volume of a clinical
segmentation network and compare it with VTK's multi-label extractor,
\texttt{vtkSurfaceNets3D} (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}
The limitations of the approach, in particular its running time relative to
the exact-predicate construction and the behavior of the perturbation at
exact coincidences, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Probability interpolation and the argmax model.} The partition
model of Section~\ref{sec:setting} appears in the multi-material literature
with Hege et al.~\cite{hege1997}, who place per-label probabilities at each
grid vertex, interpolate them independently within a cell, and assign each
inner point to the region of maximal probability, i.e.\ componentwise
interpolation followed by an argmax. Since the argmax partition
of a \emph{trilinear} interpolant is bounded by curved surfaces, their method
approximates these surfaces by subdividing each cell into a $6\times6\times6$
block of subcells, labeling each subcell by the argmax and taking the
boundaries between subcells as the surface. The result depends only on the
corner labels, so it is precomputed once per corner configuration and stored
in a table. The table covers up to three
labels, because four would require $65\,536$ entries, and the probabilities
are integers, so that edge crossings lie at midpoints. The authors leave the
continuous case open; a follow-up paper~\cite{stalling1998} addresses it by
shifting vertices for anti-aliasing, which is a smoothing step applied to the
case-table output.

\paragraph{Exact multi-material extraction.} Du et al.~\cite{du2022} compute
the argmax partition of Section~\ref{sec:setting} exactly on a tetrahedral grid,
for the piecewise-linear interpolant and for any number of labels, recovering
the non-manifold curve network, the adjacency of the patches meeting along it,
and the partitioned regions. A later paper adapts the grid to the junctions as
well as to the surfaces~\cite{ju2024}. We adopt the piecewise-linear
interpolant for the reason they give, that each logit is affine within a
cell, so that the partition becomes an arrangement whose corners have
closed-form expressions. Section~\ref{sec:further} and \supp{sec:curved-supp}
measure the error of this choice on curved interfaces.

The robustness of their construction comes from the vertex representation. A
vertex is encoded by the planes defining it and compared through a barycentric
predicate in exact arithmetic, which makes the combinatorial output provably
correct on inputs where floating-point implementations fail. Since an exact
predicate is a sign test, this representation provides no coordinate that
could be differentiated. In our construction each
vertex is an explicit closed-form linear solve, so that its position is a
differentiable function of the nodal logits, and exact coincidences are
resolved by a deterministic perturbation (Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy})
rather than by exact arithmetic. The perturbation is a weaker guarantee than
exact arithmetic, and when no gradient is needed their construction is the
better choice.

Their method could also be made differentiable by running the exact
construction, reading off the planes that define each output vertex, and
solving those systems again inside an automatic-differentiation framework.
This hybrid would return the same vertices as ours, with their combinatorial
guarantee. It would run external exact-arithmetic code at every optimization
step and keep that code's output matched to the computation graph. The
construction here is a single operation within the differentiation framework.
Its combinatorial decisions are floating-point comparisons made robust by the
perturbation, and on every generic input the two constructions agree vertex
for vertex (Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}).

\paragraph{Case-table methods and current tooling.} The construction of
Hege et al.\ also underpins multiple material marching cubes~\cite{wu2003}, separating-surface
methods~\cite{nielson1997}, non-manifold polygonization~\cite{bloomenthal1995},
Delaunay-based multi-material meshing~\cite{boltcheva2009},
SurfaceNets~\cite{frisken2022} and multi-material dual
contouring~\cite{mmdc2023}, and CGAL implements it today~\cite{cgalmesh3,
cgal2024}. In that pipeline triple curves are \emph{detected and then
protected} as one-dimensional constraints during Delaunay refinement, not
computed as part of the arrangement they belong to, and no stage carries a
derivative. On an integer segmentation there
is no underlying continuous field, so any reconstruction is a modeling choice
and exactness is not defined. The exactness claims in this paper are relative
to the interpolant of a continuous logit field, such as the output of a
network.

\paragraph{Interface reconstruction from volume fractions.}
Interface reconstruction for multi-material hydrodynamics starts from the
volume fraction of each material in each cell, and it meets the same
difficulty at junctions. Nested dissection carves materials off one at a time and is
order dependent~\cite{kucharik2010}, and moment-of-fluid~\cite{dyadechko2008}
removes the dependence by trying every order of the materials. Kucharik et
al.~\cite{kucharik2010} state the limit: ``some interface configurations may not
be reproducible by any particular order'', the example being four materials
meeting at a point, where sequential carving loses the cross-shaped interface.
This is the case of four labels tying at a point
(Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}), which our
construction extracts directly because it imposes no order on the labels.
Those methods reproduce per-cell fractions and are deliberately local, so
their interfaces are not continuous across cells. We reproduce the argmax
partition of a field exactly, with globally conforming interfaces
(Proposition~\ref{prop:conform}), and offer no prescribed-volume guarantee
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The Voronoi Implicit Interface
Method~\cite{saye2011, saye2012} also recovers junctions from a single
function, by a Voronoi construction on the level sets of one unsigned
distance function. That construction is a regularization applied after
extraction, and its authors report it as first-order accurate at junctions.

\paragraph{Power diagrams.} Schofield et al.~\cite{schofield2008,
schofield2009} avoid the material ordering by fitting to the fractions the weights $w_k$
of a \emph{power diagram}, which gives each point $x$ to the site $c_k$
minimizing $\lVert x - c_k\rVert^2 - w_k$, and observe that the resulting subcells
are convex. This convexity is Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}, and it is the
reason power diagrams serve as the exactness benchmark here. Where power diagrams are the intended output, volume-constrained
weight solvers~\cite{degoes2015} are mature, and Numerow et
al.~\cite{numerow2024} make the construction differentiable by recovering a
cell vertex as the intersection of three bisector planes through a $3\times3$
inverse with closed-form first \emph{and} second derivatives. In that setting
theirs is the method to use, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:affine}
our output coincides with it. Their interfaces are bisector planes of sites.
Our input is an arbitrary field sampled at grid nodes, and the argmax
partition of such a field is in general not the power diagram of any set of
sites.

\paragraph{Differentiable isosurface extractors.} The differentiable
\emph{isosurface} extractors take a scalar field, and several state
their output guarantee in two-label terms: FlexiCubes~\cite{shen2023} and
TetWeave~\cite{tetweave2025} guarantee watertight, two-manifold,
intersection-free output, which a triple curve violates. In
Section~\ref{sec:flexicubes} we compare against FlexiCubes directly, on
extraction, on optimization, and on three regions requested one at a time,
where its cell-averaged vertices round the shared wall differently from each
side, so that the result is not a partition. MeshSDF~\cite{remelli2020} and
MeshUDF~\cite{guillard2022} differentiate vertex positions with respect to a
scalar field through a custom backward pass. MIND~\cite{mind2025} also
builds a multi-label field, from an
unsigned distance field (UDF), and extracts it with ``the M3C method with minor
modifications'', that is, with multiple material marching
cubes~\cite{wu2003} and so the per-cell case analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:failure}. The labeling is constructed
by an $\alpha$-expansion graph cut, which is a discrete optimization, and a
subsequent refinement pass moves the emitted vertices at fixed connectivity by
minimizing the unsigned distance at each vertex subject to a Laplacian term
computed separately within each label group. The error we measure in
Section~\ref{sec:failure} lies in the combinatorics, which a pass at fixed
connectivity leaves unchanged.

\paragraph{Compositional per-object fields.} A separate line reaches
multi-region geometry by composing one field per object in place of labeling
one field. ObjectSDF~\cite{objectsdf2022} represents a scene
as one signed distance function per object, whose pointwise minimum is the
scene field, which is the same construction as an argmax of logits up to sign,
and ContactField~\cite{contactfield2024} predicts occupancy together with an
instance-identity field. Most directly, S2MDF~\cite{s2mdf2026} constrains
\emph{vector-valued} signed distance fields, which is our setting under another
name, so that objects provably do not interpenetrate. These methods make
independently predicted per-object fields mutually consistent, which is a
separate problem from extracting a consistent field. S2MDF meshes its constrained field with marching cubes or marching
tetrahedra, and an extractor for the partition, such as ours, can take that
step, in particular where objects touch (Sections~\ref{sec:failure}
and~\ref{sec:contact}).
Meshing each object independently produces a contact region twice, as two
coincident sheets, and its bounding curve is not among the outputs. Our
extractor emits that region once and computes its triple curves together
with the rest of the arrangement (Section~\ref{sec:contact}). A restriction
of the partition model is that a point carries exactly one label, so
overlapping or fuzzy memberships cannot be represented.

The exact argmax partition is computed by Du et
al.~\cite{du2022}, and differentiable extraction is available for two labels.
A 2026 treatment of topologically correct multi-class boundary
extraction~\cite{vira2026} states that no method guarantees topological
correctness and absence of holes for multiple classes, and solves the 2D case
only; MIND~\cite{mind2025} records the same absence from the learning side,
observing that its implementation ``generates MI [material interfaces] from pre-learned UDFs, making
the presented method primarily a zero-level set extraction algorithm'' and
that techniques learning a material interface directly from raw input are
``highly desired''. On the simulation side, immersogeometric analysis handles
multiple materials with conventional level sets~\cite{fromm2025}, and
NIFE~\cite{nife2024} differentiates elasticity on an evolving implicit domain
with respect to shape without an explicit extraction. The extractor in this
paper computes the exact partition for any number of labels and provides a
derivative.


\section{Setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}

Let $f : \Omega \to \R^K$ be a \emph{multi-label field} on a box
$\Omega \subset \R^3$, assigning to each point a vector of $K$ logits. It
partitions $\Omega$ into regions
\[
  \region{k} = \{\, x \in \Omega : f_k(x) > f_j(x) \ \ \forall j \neq k \,\},
  \qquad k \in \labels = \{1,\dots,K\},
\]
and $\lab(x) = \arg\max_k f_k(x)$ is the label at $x$. This is the
representation that a segmentation network already produces, and it includes
the two-label case ($K=2$, with $f_1 - f_2$ the usual scalar field). The $f_k$
may be any real scores, such as a network's logits or class probabilities,
since only their differences matter.

The features of interest are the strata of the partition: interface
\emph{patches} $\partial\region{k} \cap \partial\region{l}$, \emph{triple
curves} where three closures meet, and \emph{quadruple points} where four do.
The regions with these features form the \emph{arrangement} of the field.

The construction is posed on a conforming tetrahedral background grid obtained by Kuhn--Freudenthal
subdivision of a uniform grid of $n^3$ cubes, at resolution $n$, into $6n^3$ tetrahedra~\cite{freudenthal1942,
kuhn1960}. Each cube is cut along its main diagonal into
the six tetrahedra given by the six orders in which the three axis steps can be
taken (Figure~\ref{fig:tetrahedra}, left). Because every cube is cut by the
same rule, the grid is conforming. On a shared cube face both neighbors induce
the same diagonal, so the two sides agree on the two triangles of that face
and no welding step is needed (Figure~\ref{fig:tetrahedra}, center). Writing
$L_v = f(v) \in \R^K$ for the logits
sampled at node $v$, the \emph{interpolant} $\interp$ is the componentwise $P_1$
interpolant: on a tetrahedron $\tet$ with nodes $v_0,\dots,v_3$ and barycentric
coordinates $\lambda$,
\begin{equation}
  \interp^{\tet}(x) \;=\; \sum_{a=0}^{3} \lambda_a(x)\, L_{v_a}
  \;\in\; \R^K .
  \label{eq:interp}
\end{equation}
Because $\lambda$ is affine, each $\interp_k^\tet$ is affine on $\tet$, and
because adjacent tetrahedra share the nodes of their common face, $\interp$ is
continuous across faces. Since each $\interp_k^\tet$ is affine, a tie of $m$
labels on $\tet$ is a system of $m-1$ linear equations, which
is solved on an edge for $m=2$, on a face for $m=3$ and in the interior for
$m=4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:tetrahedra}, right). The object we extract is the
argmax partition of $\interp$,
\begin{equation}
  \dregion{k} = \{\, x : \interp_k(x) \ge \interp_j(x) \ \ \forall j \,\},
  \label{eq:dregion}
\end{equation}
and its shared boundaries are the output. Since the inequality is weak, the
$\dregion{k}$, unlike the $\region{k}$, are closed and meet along their shared
interfaces; they have disjoint interiors and cover $\Omega$, and we use the
word ``partition'' in this sense throughout. Apart from the perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}, the replacement of $f$ by $\interp$ is the only
approximation in the method; all later steps are exact.

\section{Corner-label case analysis in three dimensions}
\label{sec:failure}

The standard way to extract an isosurface is to classify each cell by the
labels at its corners and look up the resulting patch structure in a
table~\cite{lorensen1987, doi1991}. Extended to $K$ labels on tetrahedra, the natural
table follows from a counting argument. It places one crossing on each edge whose
two endpoints differ, one triple point inside each face showing three distinct
corner labels, and one quadruple point inside each tetrahedron showing four.
We call this corner-label case analysis. The corner labels decide which
features a cell has, and Newton's method on the field then locates each one. We show that it is incorrect in three dimensions and explain why
refinement does not remove the error.

\paragraph{The case-table assumption.} A face with corner labels $\{k,l,p\}$
is assumed to contain the point where $\interp_k = \interp_l = \interp_p$.
Such a point exists, since three affine functions on a plane generically tie
somewhere, but it need not lie \emph{inside the face}. When it lies outside,
all three regions appear in the face without meeting there, because one of
them separates the other two, and the argmax partition of the face has no
interior vertex. Figure~\ref{fig:multifeature} shows an example. In this situation the
case table places a triple point outside the cell that claimed it and builds
patches on it.

\paragraph{Behavior under refinement.} In 2D, misread faces become rare
under refinement, because three regions meet only at isolated points, so a
fixed number of triangles is affected and their share vanishes as the grid
gets finer. In 3D, three regions meet along a curve, and the share of
three-label faces that are misread does not decrease. Consider a small
neighborhood of a point of the curve where no two of the three patches are
parallel. As the cell size $h$ shrinks, the field approaches its
linearization there. The curve becomes a straight line and the patches become
flat half-planes meeting along it. Every cell then sees the same
configuration at every resolution, up to a shift, so the share of misread
faces approaches a constant. The constant is not zero, because a range of
shifts produces a misread face. The number of misread faces thus grows as
$\Theta(h^{-1})$, like the number of faces along the curve. Relative to all
faces of the grid, which grow as $\Theta(h^{-3})$, they become rare, but all
of them lie on the junctions, so the error there does not decrease.

\paragraph{Measured misread rate.} Table~\ref{tab:failure} confirms this
behavior on a five-site power diagram. The number of three-label faces grows
as $205$, $402$, $608$ across resolutions $16$, $32$, $48$, which is close to
the predicted $\Theta(h^{-1})$, while the fraction of them containing no
interior triple point moves from $49.8\%$ to $47.4\%$, non-monotonically and
without approaching zero. The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:failure} repeats
the sweep on a second, unrelated power diagram, where the rate is $40.0\%$,
$42.1\%$, $43.8\%$, $42.0\%$ over resolutions $8$ to $48$, i.e.\ flat or
slightly rising. Figure~\ref{fig:failure} shows the geometric consequence.
On a piecewise-planar power-diagram cell, which a correct extractor recovers
exactly at any resolution, the corner-label method has a relative volume
error of $7.3\times10^{-2}$ at resolution $48$ and converges at a rate of
$0.47$ from resolution $8$ to $48$ (Table~\ref{tab:exact}).

\paragraph{Scope of the result.} This analysis applies to input that gives every
node a score for each material, such as the class probabilities or logits of
a segmentation network, the fields of a power diagram, or any field one
intends to optimize. Taking the largest score at each node gives a label map,
with one material per node, but the scores also say where between the nodes
the materials meet, and the case table does not use that information. If the input is only a
label map, as in a stored segmentation, the input does not say where between
the nodes the surface lies, so any table that covers every combination of
corner labels is as good as any other, and the integer-label methods of
Section~\ref{sec:related} are not affected. Marching cubes has a known fix for a related problem, the
asymptotic decider~\cite{nielson1991}, which uses the interpolated values to
choose when the corner signs fit more than one surface. On a face with three
corner labels, the labels do not determine whether the three regions meet
inside the face, so a rule of this kind must also use the values. The method
of Section~\ref{sec:method} does so for every tie, solving for the point
where the labels tie and testing whether that point lies in the face.

\section{Method: direct extraction of the arrangement}
\label{sec:method}

We build the arrangement of Equation~\eqref{eq:dregion} in four steps, given
in full in Algorithm~\ref{alg:extract}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \emph{Sampling.} We evaluate the field at the grid nodes and
displace the logits by a small perturbation, so that no two are
exactly equal (Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}).
\item[(ii)] \emph{Solving.} For every set of $m = 2, 3, 4$ labels that could
tie on a grid edge, face or tetrahedron, we compute where they tie. This is an
$(m-1)\times(m-1)$ linear system in the node logits, whose solution is the
position of the tie in barycentric coordinates (Section~\ref{sec:solve}). The
number of labels $K$ is arbitrary, but $m$ stops at $4$, since setting $m$ values
equal takes $m-1$ equations (one, $\interp_k = \interp_l$, for two labels), and
a point in 3D satisfies at most three independent ones.
\item[(iii)] \emph{Filtering.} A tie is a vertex of the output only if two
conditions hold: \emph{containment}, the tie lies inside its edge, face or
tetrahedron, and \emph{dominance}, no other label is larger there. A label
that is larger \emph{beats} the set. The label sets we test in a cell start from the labels
on its boundary and grow until no set changes (Section~\ref{sec:candidates}).
\item[(iv)] \emph{Assembly.} In each tetrahedron, the vertices of the
interface between two labels are the corners of a convex polygon, which we
sort by angle and fan into triangles (Section~\ref{sec:assembly}). Inside a
tetrahedron a triple curve is a straight segment, which joins the two
vertices of the tetrahedron that share the same three labels; the segments
together form the triple curves as polylines.
\end{enumerate}
Sections~\ref{sec:gradients} to~\ref{sec:cost} then cover the gradients,
degenerate input, tolerances and cost.

Du et al.~\cite{du2022} (Section~\ref{sec:related}) build the same arrangement
incrementally, and their construction is the more robust of the two, because
it decides every comparison exactly. Our construction makes these comparisons
in floating point. Each vertex is an explicit formula in the node logits, and
the output is differentiable. Every grid entity needs the same work, and the
whole grid is processed in a fixed number of batched operations.

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
\caption{Exact arrangement of the $P_1$ interpolant of the logits. Each
\textbf{for all} loop is over independent entities and runs as one batched
array operation; the \textbf{repeat} loop runs at most $K$ times. No step
consults a case table. All decisions are made on a detached copy of $L$ and
only the positions carry gradients (Section~\ref{sec:gradients}); the
comparisons are made with the tolerance of Section~\ref{sec:predicates}.}
\label{alg:extract}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require field $f:\Omega\to\R^K$, resolution $n$, perturbation $\varepsilon$
\Ensure triangles tagged by ordered label pair; triple curves as segments
\State build a conforming tetrahedral grid with nodes $V$
\State $L \gets f(V)$, displaced by $\varepsilon$
  \Comment{Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}}
\State $\lab(v) \gets \arg\max_k L_v[k]$
\ForAll{edges $(v_0,v_1)$ with $\lab(v_0) \neq \lab(v_1)$ and label pairs $\{k,l\}$}
  \State $d \gets L[k] - L[l]$, \; $t \gets d_0/(d_0 - d_1)$
  \If{$0 < t < 1$ and no label beats $\{k,l\}$ at $t$}
    \State emit crossing
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\For{$m = 3, 4$}
  \Comment{cells: faces, then tetrahedra}
  \State seed each cell's label set from its boundary
  \Repeat
    \ForAll{cells and label sets $S$, $|S| = m$}
      \State solve $A(L)\,\lambda' = b(L)$
        \Comment{\eqref{eq:solve}}
      \If{$\lambda'$ is in the cell and a label beats $S$ there}
        \State add the largest label there to the cell's label set
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \Until{no label set grew}
  \Comment{Section~\ref{sec:candidates}}
  \State emit every tie that is in its cell and not beaten
\EndFor
\ForAll{tetrahedra and label pairs}
  \State sort the patch's vertices by angle and fan
    \Comment{Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}}
\EndFor
\State join the two vertices of each tetrahedron that share a label triple
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Closed-form corner solves}
\label{sec:solve}

Every vertex of the output is a single point and is fixed by three
equations. If $m$ labels tie there, the tie gives $m-1$ of them. The rest
come from the tetrahedron $\tet$. Lying on one of its faces is one more
equation, the plane of that face, and lying on an edge, where two faces meet,
is two. A tie of two labels gives a vertex on an edge, a tie of three on a
face, and a tie of four inside the tetrahedron. There are three
kinds of vertex, and at most four labels tie at one ($m \le 4$;
\suppp{sec:features-supp}):
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
  \text{on a grid edge} & \interp_k = \interp_l & \text{crossing} \\
  \text{on a grid face} & \interp_k = \interp_l = \interp_p & \text{triple point} \\
  \text{inside a tetrahedron} & \interp_k = \interp_l = \interp_p = \interp_q
    & \text{quadruple point.}
\end{array}
\]
For a given label set the tie is unique, since the equations are affine and,
on a generic field (Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}), independent. An edge has
at most one crossing per label pair and a face at most one triple point per
label triple. Different label sets can each contribute one. An edge can
carry several crossings and a face several triple points. These are the configurations of Figure~\ref{fig:multifeature},
which a table with one feature per cell cannot express.

Across the five test fields of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} at
resolution $20$ there are $1\,764$
multi-crossing edges and $255$ multi-triple-point faces
(\suppp{sec:features-supp}). Dual contouring~\cite{ju2002} and its
multi-material extension~\cite{mmdc2023} do without a table but still place
one vertex per cell, which cannot hold two triple points on one face. That
vertex minimizes a least-squares error and in general does not satisfy the
equal-logit equations, even on affine logits, where these equations have an
exact solution.

Each vertex comes from a closed-form solve. Write the tied set as
$S = \{l_0,\dots,l_{m-1}\}$.
The point lies on the face of $\tet$ of dimension $m-1$ whose nodes carry the
logit vectors $L_0,\dots,L_{m-1}$, where the interpolated logit of label $k$
is $\sum_{a=0}^{m-1} \lambda_a L_a[k]$. Writing the point in the coordinates
of this face is what imposes the equations from $\tet$, since the other nodes
get coordinate zero. Let
$d_a^{\,r} = L_a[l_0] - L_a[l_r]$ be the difference of two tied logits at
node $a$. Then $\interp_{l_0} = \interp_{l_r}$ reads
$\sum_a \lambda_a d_a^{\,r} = 0$ for each $r = 1,\dots,m-1$. Substituting
$\lambda_0 = 1 - \sum_{a \ge 1} \lambda_a$ gives an equation affine in
$\lambda' = (\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{m-1})$,
$\sum_{a \ge 1} \lambda_a\,(d_a^{\,r} - d_0^{\,r}) = -d_0^{\,r}$, and the
$m-1$ equations together form the $(m-1) \times (m-1)$ system
\begin{align}
  A(L)\,\lambda' &= b(L), \label{eq:solve} \\
  A_{r a} &= d_a^{\,r} - d_0^{\,r}
           = \big(L_a[l_0] - L_a[l_r]\big)
             - \big(L_0[l_0] - L_0[l_r]\big), \nonumber \\
  b_r &= -d_0^{\,r} = -\big(L_0[l_0]-L_0[l_r]\big), \nonumber
\end{align}
where $A$ and $b$ are linear in the node logits. For a crossing, $m=2$,
\eqref{eq:solve} is the scalar equation $(d_1 - d_0)\,t = -d_0$, which gives
the familiar ratio $t = d_0/(d_0-d_1)$, with $d_a$ the difference of the two
logits at endpoint $a$; triple and quadruple points give $2\times2$ and
$3\times3$ systems. One such system is solved per entity and label set; a
worked example is in \supp{sec:features-supp}.

On a generic field every label set ties somewhere, but the tie is a vertex
only if it lies in its simplex \emph{and} no other label beats it there. We check the second
condition against all $K$ labels, not only those seeded from the cell
boundary, so that two cells sharing a face make the same decision about the
point on it.

\subsection{Candidate label sets}
\label{sec:candidates}

Trying all $\binom{K}{m}$ label sets in every cell would be wasteful, so we
start from the labels that appear on the cell's boundary. A label whose
region lies inside the cell without touching its edges is then missing, and
the tie it should have produced is a corner of a neighboring patch, so the
mesh has a hole.

When a tie lands inside a cell but is beaten, the label that is largest at
that point has a region in the cell and belongs in the set. We add it and
solve again until no set grows. The sets only grow, so this takes at most
$K$ passes; on the power-diagram and neural fields at resolutions $16$ to
$48$, two passes were enough. Without this step the cell of
Table~\ref{tab:exact} has holes at every resolution.
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} states that the grown set contains every
label that occupies the cell, including one whose region lies strictly
inside it.

Du et al.~\cite{du2022} keep a function in a tetrahedron unless another
function exceeds it at all four vertices. They note that this keeps some
functions that do not contribute, an extra cost they accept for
simplicity. Our seed starts smaller, so it has to be grown.

\subsection{Assembly}
\label{sec:assembly}

Each vertex belongs to the interfaces of every pair of labels in its tied set:
a crossing to one pair, a triple point to three, a quadruple point to six,
the number of ways to choose two of its tied labels.
Grouping the vertices by (tetrahedron, label pair) gives the corners of each
patch. Inside a tetrahedron $\tet$, the patch of $k$ and $l$, or $k|l$ patch, is the part of
the plane $\interp^\tet_k = \interp^\tet_l$ where no other label is larger,
that is, the plane cut by the half-spaces
$\{\interp^\tet_k \ge \interp^\tet_j\}$, each bounded by a plane because
$\interp^\tet_k - \interp^\tet_j$ is affine. Each patch is therefore a convex
polygon (Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}) and is fixed by its corners. Sorting the
corners by angle about the patch normal and fanning them gives a
correct triangulation with no case analysis, and unlike a table-driven fan it
cannot fold (\suppp{sec:assembly-supp}). For the pair $k < l$ the normal is
$\nabla(\interp^\tet_l - \interp^\tet_k)$, which is constant on $\tet$ and
points into region $l$. Since every patch is oriented by this rule, triangles
of one label pair are wound consistently across cells and face the higher label.

The triangles are thus built from pairs alone, and a triple curve is the
edge shared by three patches. We also return the triple curves as a separate
list of segments, which needs no further solve. Inside $\tet$ three labels $k$, $l$,
$p$ tie along a straight line, $\interp_k = \interp_l = \interp_p$, and the
triple curve is the part of that line where no fourth label is larger, which
is a segment. Its ends are vertices already computed, a triple point where the
line leaves $\tet$ through a face or a quadruple point where a fourth label
takes over. A generic tetrahedron therefore holds either none or exactly two
vertices whose tied set contains $\{k,l,p\}$, and the segment joins them.

\subsection{Gradient path}
\label{sec:gradients}

Vertex positions come from the linear solve \eqref{eq:solve}, so reverse-mode
automatic differentiation through $L = f_\theta(\text{nodes})$, where $\theta$
denotes the parameters of the field, gives exact
gradients (\suppp{sec:gradients-supp}). Every \emph{combinatorial}
decision, namely which labels tie, whether a tie is inside its simplex and
which label wins, is made on a detached copy of the logits, while positions
are computed with the computation graph intact. No vertex is found by
iteration. A root found by bisection, or by Newton's method unless every
iteration is differentiated, needs the implicit function theorem to recover
its gradient; here the interpolant is affine on each cell, so every root is
a linear solve.

\subsection{Degenerate configurations}
\label{sec:degeneracy}

The construction relies on strict inequalities, so it breaks on exact
coincidences, and on a regular grid these are common.
A node can lie exactly on an interface, for example when the radius of
a sphere is a whole number of grid steps. The crossing on each edge at that
node then falls on the node itself, is rejected as out of range, and leaves a
hole. A triple curve can also pass exactly through a grid edge, for example
when the geometry is aligned to round numbers. The three pairwise crossings
then coincide with each other and with a triple point on every face around
the edge, and the patches there are built from copies of one corner.

We fix both by perturbing the input~\cite{edelsbrunner1990}. Each node
logit is displaced by a small fixed amount of relative size $\varepsilon$,
determined by the node index, the label index and the largest logit
magnitude on the grid. Every
cell touching a node sees the same displacement, and neighboring cells still
agree on their shared boundary. The displacement is computed from a
detached copy of the logits and does not enter the gradients. It moves
the vertices by $O(\varepsilon)$, which at
$\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$ is about seven orders of magnitude below the
discretization error of a curved interface at our resolutions. Table~\ref{tab:exact} reports it separately, so that the method and its
perturbation can be told apart, and Section~\ref{sec:further} measures the
cost near an actual degeneracy.

\subsection{Predicates and tolerances}
\label{sec:predicates}

Three decisions in Algorithm~\ref{alg:extract} are floating-point comparisons,
where Du et al.~\cite{du2022} use exact predicates. All three use the tolerance $\delta = 10^{-12}$. A crossing is kept only if
$\delta < t < 1 - \delta$; a tie is kept only if it lies in its simplex,
$\lambda_a \ge -\delta$; and a label beats the tied set only if it is larger by
more than $\delta$. The first
discards a crossing at a node, which would leave the hole described in
Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} if the perturbation did not keep the nodes off
the interfaces. The second keeps a tie that rounding places just outside its
simplex, which would otherwise be lost. Two other constants do not decide
which generic features are kept: a $10^{-30}$ threshold discards solves with
a smaller denominator or determinant, in practice only degenerate ones, and a
$10^{-9}$ window marks the vertices on the domain boundary, where the closure
check excuses unpaired sides.

The tolerance and the perturbation leave conformity intact. Before
anything is solved, edges and faces are merged into global entities keyed by
their sorted node indices. The triple point of a face is therefore computed
once, from the three corner logits of that face in a fixed order, and
dominance is tested at that point over all $K$ labels. Both tetrahedra sharing
the face read the same result, and Proposition~\ref{prop:conform} is a
statement about this shared computation, which the tolerance does not enter.
$\delta$ is a slack on barycentric coordinates and dominance margins, and the
perturbation moves the logits before either is computed, each $L_v[k]$ by
$\mathrm{frac}_v[k]\,\varepsilon s$, where $s = \max(1, \max_{u,j} |L_u[j]|)$
and $\mathrm{frac}_v[k] \in [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ is a fixed pseudo-random
function of the node and label indices.
The $K$ values at a node lie in separate intervals of width $1/K$, so two labels tied there move
at least $\varepsilon s/K$ apart, and the crossings at the node lie more than
$\delta$ inside their edges if $\varepsilon > 2K\delta$, which
$\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$ satisfies by a factor of over $30$ for every $K$ here. Where
three labels tie, the triple points nearby can still fall within $\delta$ of a
side of their face, and a few holes can remain (\suppp{sec:degentest-supp}). The
implementation counts patches left with fewer than three corners, the symptom
of a corner rejected by $\delta$, and with the perturbation on this count is
zero on every field in this paper.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Each candidate label set costs one solve of size at most $3\times3$ and one
dominance test against all $K$ labels, so a tetrahedron with $c$ candidate
labels costs $O(\binom{c}{4}K)$ at the quadruple level and less at the levels
below. Edges are the exception. On them we test all $K(K-1)/2$ label pairs,
at $O(K^3)$ per edge, but only on edges whose endpoints differ, which are a
small fraction of the grid. Skipping the others misses no crossing, since each region
meets an edge in a single interval (Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}), so an edge
with the same label at both ends has no crossing. The incremental construction costs $O(c^3)$ on the same
tetrahedron~\cite{du2022}, so ours is asymptotically the more expensive.

In practice it is affordable because $c$ is small and does not grow with $K$.
Across the five test fields of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} at resolution
$24$, each with $82\,944$ tetrahedra, the mean candidate set holds between
$1.10$ and $1.48$ labels, and the largest set, of five labels, occurs in $23$
tetrahedra of the $K=6$ neural field. Since
every edge, face and tetrahedron needs the same work, each kind is processed
in a few batched operations and the backward pass is ordinary automatic
differentiation.

Refining a $K=6$ power diagram from $24\,576$ to
$3\,072\,000$ tetrahedra, a factor of $125$, raises the wall time from $0.07$
to $8.9$\,s. The time grows linearly with the number of tetrahedra, at $2.6$
to $2.9\,\mu$s per tetrahedron over the whole range. Raising $K$ from $3$ to
$16$ at fixed resolution costs a factor of $2.6$, while the mean candidate set
grows from $1.06$ to $1.18$ labels. Memory is about one
kilobyte per tetrahedron, or $2.9$\,GB at three million tetrahedra, which sets
the highest resolution one machine can reach. The sweep is tabulated in
\supp{sec:scaling-supp}.

\section{Guarantees}
\label{sec:guarantees}

\noindent The properties of the construction all follow from the
interpolant being affine within a cell. They are stated for exact arithmetic and a \emph{generic} field,
one in which no tie is degenerate (its system \eqref{eq:solve} is
nonsingular), no label is exactly equal to a tied set, and no vertex lies
exactly on the boundary of its simplex.
Genericity is the condition that the perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} is designed to establish, and the proofs note
where they use it. Every
patch is a convex polygon, neighboring cells agree on their shared vertices,
no label is missing from a candidate set, planar input is recovered exactly,
at most four labels meet at a vertex, the three sheets at a triple curve are
in force balance, and the output is differentiable wherever its topology is
stable. The proofs are given in
\supp{sec:proofs-supp}.

\begin{proposition}[Convexity]
\label{prop:convex}
For every tetrahedron $\tet$ and every $k$, the part $\dregion{k}\cap\tet$
of the region of Equation~\eqref{eq:dregion} is a convex polyhedron, and for every pair $k \neq l$ the patch
$\dregion{k}\cap\dregion{l}\cap\tet$ is a convex polygon.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:fan}
A patch is the convex hull of its corners. If it is two-dimensional and its
corners are distinct and in strictly convex position, sorting them by angle about the patch normal and fanning
produces a triangulation whose triangles are non-degenerate, consistently
wound, and non-overlapping. On a generic field every non-empty patch is of
this kind. Otherwise the fan can contain degenerate triangles, and a patch
left with fewer than three corners is counted by the implementation
(Section~\ref{sec:predicates}).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Conformity]
\label{prop:conform}
Let $\varphi$ be a face shared by $\tet$ and $\tet'$. The crossings and triple
points the method places on $\varphi$ are the same vertices for $\tet$ and for
$\tet'$, as floating-point values and as indices.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The method therefore needs no welding, snapping or
tolerance-based merging. We call the output closed,
or watertight, if each triangle side is used exactly twice with opposite
winding, except on triple curves and at the domain boundary. This also
requires that neither cell \emph{omits} a corner, which the proposition does
not guarantee; we verified numerically that it holds on forty randomly drawn
fields, none of them among the five test fields.

\begin{proposition}[Completeness of the candidate sets]
\label{prop:complete}
When the growth of Section~\ref{sec:candidates} stops, the candidate set of
every face and every tetrahedron contains every label whose region meets it,
including a label whose region lies strictly inside it.
\end{proposition}

\noindent In floating point the growth needs the missing label to beat a tie
by more than $\delta$, so only a region in which its label nowhere exceeds
the others by more than $\delta$ can be missed.

\begin{proposition}[Exactness on affine logits]
\label{prop:affine}
If every difference $f_k - f_l$ is affine, then
$\interp_k - \interp_l = f_k - f_l$ on $\Omega$ and the extracted complex is
the exact argmax partition of $f$ on any grid on which $f$ is generic. In
particular power diagrams, and hence Voronoi diagrams, are recovered exactly
at any such resolution; in
floating point with the perturbation off, the only error is the rounding of
the solves.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Vertex classification]
\label{prop:classify}
At most four labels tie at any vertex of the extracted complex, and a vertex at
which $m$ labels tie lies on a face of its tetrahedron of dimension $m-1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Force balance at triple curves]
\label{prop:plateau}
Let three patches meet along a triple curve of labels $\{k,l,p\}$ inside a
tetrahedron, and let $g_k = \nabla \interp^\tet_k$ be the (constant) gradients.
The three patch normals $n_{kl} = g_l - g_k$, $n_{lp} = g_p - g_l$,
$n_{pk} = g_k - g_p$ are orthogonal to the curve and satisfy
\[
  n_{kl} + n_{lp} + n_{pk} = 0 .
\]
Consequently the three dihedral angles are $120^\circ$ exactly if and only if
$\|n_{kl}\| = \|n_{lp}\| = \|n_{pk}\|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The vanishing sum is Herring's force-balance condition at a triple
junction~\cite{herring1951, taylor1976}, with the lengths of the normals in
the role of the surface tensions, so the extractor satisfies the balance
identically, and the $120^\circ$ angles of equal tensions appear whenever
the three lengths are equal. Since the identity holds by construction,
checking it on the normals is not informative. Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}
instead measures the angle between the triangles emitted on a curved
interface.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Differentiability]
\label{prop:diff}
Fix $\theta_0$ near which $\theta \mapsto f_\theta$ is continuously
differentiable and at which every predicate deciding the structure holds
strictly: no tie is degenerate
($\det A \neq 0$ in \eqref{eq:solve} for every enumerated label set), no vertex lies
on the boundary of its simplex, every non-tied label is strictly beaten, and
every node has a single largest logit. Then the combinatorial
structure is constant in a neighborhood of $\theta_0$, every vertex position
is a differentiable function of $\theta$ there, and reverse-mode automatic
differentiation realizes its exact Jacobian.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The excluded set consists of the parameters at which a vertex
enters or leaves a cell or a tie becomes degenerate. There the structure
changes, and a loss on the vertices need not be differentiable. If
$f_\theta$ is analytic in $\theta$ and no predicate holds with equality for
all $\theta$, the set has measure zero.
With the perturbation on, its displacement is held constant during
differentiation; since it scales with $\max_{u,j}|L_u[j]|$, the computed
Jacobian then differs from that of the perturbed positions by
$O(\varepsilon)$.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We measure correctness with $28$ automated checks, most of them against
closed-form answers, all of which pass, and in Section~\ref{sec:headtohead} against
the reference implementation of Du et al.~\cite{du2022}. We use three families of test field
with exact answers:

\begin{description}
\item[Power diagrams.] The logits $f_k(x) = w_k - \lVert x - c_k\rVert^2$
differ by affine functions, so by
Proposition~\ref{prop:affine} the correct output is the power diagram itself
at every resolution. The volume, facet areas and corner positions of each cell
are computed analytically, which provides ground truth to machine precision.
\item[Spherical shells.] Concentric regions with exact radii. The interface
areas are known, but since the geometry is curved, exactness is impossible and
we measure the convergence order instead.
\item[Symmetric sector fans.] $K$ regions meeting at a point along the
directions of a regular simplex, so that every dihedral angle is known to be
$120^\circ$.
\end{description}

\noindent In addition, neural fields (randomly initialized MLPs on Fourier
features, $K=4$ and $K=6$) are included wherever ground truth is not required,
to check that the construction does not depend on the field being analytic.
Together these give the five test fields used throughout: a power diagram
($K=5$), a sector fan ($K=4$), spherical shells ($K=4$) and the two neural
fields (\suppp{sec:fields-supp}), all on $\Omega = [-1,1]^3$. All arithmetic
is carried out in float64.
Section~\ref{sec:further} also reports a sensitivity analysis on an
edge case, five regions meeting at a point.

\subsection{Exactness on a power-diagram cell}

Table~\ref{tab:exact} contains our central quantitative result. The input is
a power diagram of $15$ sites whose central cell is a bounded polyhedron with
$14$ facets and $24$ corners, located well inside the domain, with volume
$0.4380$ and surface area $3.102$; Figure~\ref{fig:failure} shows the field
(left) and the cell (second and third panels). We extract it on
the same grids at resolutions $8$ to $48$ in three ways. The baseline is corner-label case analysis, the per-cell
lookup used by multi-material marching cubes and marching tetrahedra

switched on, as it is by default. Each output is scored by the relative error $|Q - Q^\ast|/Q^\ast$ of the
volume and surface area $Q$ of the extracted cell against the closed-form values

the eight entries are bit-exact, and the result

errors are of the size of the perturbation. The corner-label method has
errors of $7$--$20\%$, and its volume error decreases from resolution $8$ to
$48$ at a rate of $0.47$.

\subsection{Further validation}
\label{sec:further}

The remaining checks, each on the field best suited to it, are reported in
full in supplementary Sections~\sref{sec:degentest-supp}
to~\sref{sec:checks-supp}, and the gradient check in Section~\sref{sec:gradients-supp}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analysis on an edge case
(\suppp{sec:degentest-supp}).} The field is a power diagram of five sites
placed on a common sphere. The center of that sphere is equidistant from all
five sites, so five regions meet there, a coincidence with no two-label
analog. Pushing one site outward by $t$ splits this point into two quadruple
points about $t$ apart. The exact position of every quadruple point is computed by
an independent enumeration
(Figure~\sref{fig:degensetup}, Table~\sref{tab:degeneracy}). The perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} moves the logits by a fixed $\varepsilon$. Close to
a degeneracy a vertex could in principle move by much more, by $\varepsilon/t$. The
measured error, the largest distance from a true quadruple point to the nearest
extracted one, is instead at most $1.8\varepsilon$ for every $t$ down to
$10^{-10}$, because each quadruple point solves its own $3\times3$
system, which the fifth label does not enter. Once $\varepsilon$ is ten times
the separation of the two points, their positions are still accurate to
$O(\varepsilon)$, but their relative arrangement is no longer resolved. This
bounds $\varepsilon$ from above, and the condition
$\varepsilon > 2K\delta$ of Section~\ref{sec:predicates} bounds it from below;
the default of $10^{-9}$ satisfies both. The test
measures geometric error on one controlled family; the exact predicates of
Du et al.~\cite{du2022} guarantee correct combinatorial structure on every
input.

\paragraph{Our vertices lie on their interfaces
(\suppp{sec:pointwise-supp}).} The field is again the cell of
Table~\ref{tab:exact} and Figure~\ref{fig:failure}, at resolution $48$. It is
extracted by our method, by corner-label case analysis, and by
\texttt{vtkSurfaceNets3D}~\cite{vtk}, the multi-label extractor of VTK,
which takes a label map and is therefore given the argmax of the
same field. For each vertex we measure its distance from the interface of
the two largest logits there, which has a closed form since each $f_i-f_j$ is affine
(Figure~\sref{fig:accuracy}). Our method
places each of its $8\,819$ vertices on its interface to machine precision.
Corner-label case analysis places most vertices exactly, but $2.0\%$ are off
the interface, the worst by $1.93$ grid cells, and this worst case does not
converge, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:failure}. All of the
SurfaceNets vertices are off the interface, by $3.3\times10^{-3}$ ($0.08$ grid
cells) at the median at resolution $48$, and they converge at roughly first order. Its input
is a label map, which does not hold the values that located the interface.

\paragraph{Single-value checks.}
The fields are drawn in Figure~\sref{fig:testfields}.\\
\textit{Convergence on curved interfaces.}
Spherical shells (\suppp{sec:curved-supp}): the area of a sphere converges at a
fitted rate of $2.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}, right), the order a $P_1$
interpolant supports and the order of marching cubes on a two-label field. The
shells have no junctions; convergence at curved junctions is measured in
Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}.\\
\textit{Tied logits at emitted vertices.}
The five fields of Figure~\sref{fig:testfields}
(\suppp{sec:consistency-supp}): as a consistency check on the implementation we
evaluate the interpolant at each emitted vertex independently of the solve of
Equation~\eqref{eq:solve}; the logits that are supposed to tie differ by at
most $2.8\times10^{-15}$.\\
\textit{Closure and orientation.}
Five fields at two resolutions, up to $45\,446$ triangles
(\suppp{sec:closure-supp}): there are no unpaired sides, no inconsistently
oriented sides and no patches with too few corners.\\
\textit{Gradients against finite differences} (\suppp{sec:gradients-supp}).
Automatic differentiation agrees with central finite differences to
$1.1\times10^{-8}$ on a power diagram differentiated with respect to all sites
and weights, and to $7.9\times10^{-8}$ on weights sampled from every layer of
a smaller neural field.\\
\textit{Dihedral angles at triple curves.}
Symmetric sector fan (\suppp{sec:plateau-supp}): over $155$ triple-curve
segments the worst deviation from $120^\circ$ is $1.4\times10^{-14}$ degrees.
Proposition~\ref{prop:plateau} says that this is an identity on a sector fan.
On a curved interface in Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble} the angles lie within
$0.4^\circ$ of the exact value and converge at rate $2.05$.

\subsection{Agreement with the exact baseline}
\label{sec:headtohead}

We also compare against the public code of Du et al.~\cite{du2022}, whose
output is provably correct. Wherever the field is generic, agreement with
it checks our output against a known correct result from an independent
implementation.

Both implementations read the same tetrahedra, passed to the reference
through its \texttt{tetMeshFile} input, so that the algorithm is the only
difference. We added a loader path so that it can read per-vertex values for
the fields that have no closed form; this does not change the arrangement
code. The
perturbation is switched off, because a displacement that the
reference does not apply would guarantee a mismatch at exactly the
configurations of interest. Details are in \supp{sec:headtohead-supp}.

We ran eight configurations: four built from the reference's own analytic primitives
(spheres and planes, Figure~\sref{fig:dusetup}); a power diagram and a $K=6$ neural field
(Figure~\sref{fig:testfields}) delivered as per-vertex values; the clinical liver field with its exact coincidences
removed, on $663\,552$ tetrahedra; and a control, in which a field that the
loader can evaluate analytically is sent through the per-vertex path instead,
so that the loader addition is tested on its own.

On all eight configurations the two implementations agree on every count, namely vertices, interface polygons,
patches, labels and the set of interfaces present, and the symmetric
nearest-neighbor distance between the two vertex sets is at most $1.1\times10^{-14}$
(\suppp{sec:headtohead-supp}, Table~\sref{tab:headtohead}).

The one exception is the same liver field used as given, with the
coincidences of Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy}. Ten of its $117\,649$ nodes carry
an exact argmax tie, and eight more are tied up to rounding. The reference
computes this degenerate partition exactly, with vertices on those nodes. Ours
leaves holes at them with the perturbation off; with it on, each vertex on an
exactly tied node becomes a cluster of nearby vertices. The vertex counts therefore differ by
under $0.2\%$, entirely at those nodes, while patches, labels and the
interfaces present agree. With the perturbation on, every vertex of either mesh
lies within $1.7\times10^{-7}$\,mm of one of the other, and removing the
coincidences from the input restores exact agreement. The reference is
faster. Its arrangement takes $12$--$148$\,ms and ours $97$\,ms--$1.1$\,s,
in Python against optimized C++ with a precomputed lookup table.

\subsection{Comparison with FlexiCubes}
\label{sec:flexicubes}

FlexiCubes~\cite{shen2023} is a differentiable two-label extractor. It
takes one scalar field on a grid, places one dual vertex per
cell as a weighted average of the edge crossings of that cell, and guarantees
watertight two-manifold output. Section~\ref{sec:related} stated that a
triple curve lies outside that guarantee and that our construction includes
the two-label setting. We test both statements with three experiments:
extraction from a fixed field, optimization of a field, and three regions
requested one at a time, the only way to give them to a single-field
extractor, known as one-versus-rest. Their inputs are drawn in
Figure~\sref{fig:fcsetup}; the protocol and full tables
are in \supp{sec:flexicubes-supp}. Their extractor runs unmodified in float32,
the precision it was written for, and ours in float64.

\paragraph{Extraction at $K=2$.} An analytic sphere and an analytic torus are
sampled as two-label fields on matched grids with identical node and cell
counts, at resolutions $16$ to $64$, and each extractor is run once without
optimization, FlexiCubes with its default weights. Both outputs are scored
against the analytic surface, by the relative area error and by the distance
of points drawn uniformly by area from the mesh. Our area error is $2.4$ to
$3.5$ times lower, and our sampled points lie closer to the
analytic surface by a factor of two to $2.2$ in the root mean square and
$1.2$ to $1.7$ in the worst case. Our mesh has about three times as many
triangles. Scoring vertices instead would favor our method further in the
root mean square, since a dual vertex need not lie on the surface
(Table~\sref{tab:fcaccuracy}).

\paragraph{Optimization at $K=2$.} One scalar grid of $33^3$ values,
initialized to a sphere, is optimized through each extractor under the same
Chamfer objective against a point cloud drawn from an analytic ellipsoid; only
the grid values are optimized, and each method is tuned over learning rate and
the weight of a smoothness penalty on the grid. The objective is defined on the point cloud, but accuracy is
scored against the analytic surface. Our final objective is $29\%$ higher.
On the true surface the difference is larger. The FlexiCubes
surface lies $0.043$ grid cells from the ellipsoid in the root mean square
and ours $0.274$ (up to $1.86$ over five seeds of the sampler); in the worst
case the distances are $0.16$ and $3.6$ cells. Part of our error comes from small separate pieces of
surface that appear away from the target during the descent; without them it
is $0.17$ cells, still four times that of FlexiCubes. The rest is consistent
with field noise, which our method reproduces and their averaging damps
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). On a target that additionally requires a change of genus,
that is, a hole opened through the surface, neither
method succeeds, and both stall at $1.6$ to $2.0$ cells (Table~\sref{tab:fcopt}).

\paragraph{Three regions by one-versus-rest.} The three regions are those of
the standard double bubble (Figure~\sref{fig:fcsetup}, bottom): the exterior
and two equal lobes that meet along a flat wall and a circular triple curve.
We run FlexiCubes once per lobe $k$, on the field $\max_{j\neq k} f_j - f_k$
of that lobe versus the rest, at resolutions $16$ to $48$. Each run returns
a closed surface. We then test whether those two surfaces, with the exterior,
partition space. For this, $40\,000$ sample points are classified by winding number,
counting points inside both lobes (overlap) and points of the true lobes inside
neither (gap).
FlexiCubes claims $0.90\%$ of the domain twice and leaves $2.9\%$ unclaimed
(Table~\sref{tab:fcjunction}). Refinement lowers one at the expense of the
other, and the share of points labeled wrongly decreases only from
$3.9\%$ to $3.3\%$. The two runs produce no shared wall and no triple curve.
A dual vertex averages the crossings of its cell, and near the junction that
average straddles a crease in the field; the two extractions of the same wall
therefore do not coincide, and the crease does not flatten under refinement.
The same two fields, passed to our extractor one at a time, give overlap zero
at every resolution, since interpolating a maximum gives at least the maximum
of the interpolants, and a labeling error that decreases from $0.67\%$ to
$0.08\%$. In one pass, our method emits each wall once and returns the triple
curve as an edge, which the contact operator of Section~\ref{sec:contact}
requires.

The first two applications are optimizations in 3D whose loss passes through
a junction and whose answer is known in closed form: the double bubble
(Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}), where interface area is minimized at equal
volumes, and contact between three solid objects (Section~\ref{sec:contact}),
whose contact areas are driven to prescribed values. The third
(Section~\ref{sec:realdata}) applies the extractor to measured data, the
probability volume of a segmentation network, for which no closed form exists.

Sections~\ref{sec:doublebubble} and~\ref{sec:contact} use the
same field. The background is label $0$, with $f_0 \equiv 0$, and each ball is a channel
$f_k(x) = r_k^2 - \lVert x - c_k\rVert^2$. Ball $k$ then meets the background
on the sphere of center $c_k$ and radius $r_k$, and two balls meet on a plane,
because $f_i - f_j$ is affine. The rim of that wall, where both balls and the
background meet, is a triple curve. We place all balls generically, so that
no wall or curve lies on a grid plane or grid line.

Both optimizations differentiate the area
of such walls, so we first check that area and its derivative against closed
form. For two balls of radius $r$ whose centers are $s$ apart, the wall is a
disk of radius $\sqrt{r^2-s^2/4}$ on the bisector plane, and its area, the
derivative of the area with respect to $s$, and its rim are known in closed
form. From deep overlap to the loss of contact, the area is
accurate to $1.6\%$, its derivative to $0.7\%$ and the rim to $0.7\%$, as long
as the disk radius exceeds five grid cells. The error depends only on that
radius, because inside the wall the surface is exact and all of the error
lies in the position of the rim. The
derivative by automatic differentiation agrees with a central difference
to $1.8\times10^{-10}$ relative error, the truncation error of
the difference. Refining from $24^3$ to $128^3$, the area converges at an
average order of $1.99$.

\subsection{Minimal interface area: the double bubble}
\label{sec:doublebubble}

Two regions of equal volume share a wall, and their total interface area is to
be minimized. By the double bubble theorem~\cite{hutchings2002} the minimizer
is the standard double bubble: two spherical caps of equal radius $R$ whose
centers are $R$ apart, separated by a flat disk on their bisector plane, with
the three surfaces meeting along a circle at equal angles. We test whether
gradient descent through our extractor reaches this shape from a lopsided
start, and whether the corner-label extractor of Section~\ref{sec:failure}
does under the same loss.

The two lobes are the balls $k=1,2$ of the field above, and the
outside is the background, so the standard double bubble is a member of this
family.

We minimize
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{A}{\bar V^{2/3}}
    + w_b \bigg(\frac{V_1 - V_2}{\bar V}\bigg)^{\!2}
    + w_s \bigg(\frac{\bar V}{V^\star} - 1\bigg)^{\!2},
  \quad \bar V = \tfrac{1}{2}(V_1 + V_2),
  \label{eq:bubbleloss}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the total interface area, $V_k$ the volume of lobe $k$ and
$V^\star$ a target size. The first term, which we call the objective, is the
usual isoperimetric ratio in three dimensions, the area with the size divided out. The second keeps the two
volumes equal, and the third keeps the overall size near $V^\star$. We use
$w_s = 20$ and raise $w_b$ from $10$ to $300$ during the run, since a large
$w_b$ from the start would fix the volumes before the shape has moved.

Two simpler losses do not reach the double bubble. A mesh of a convex surface lies slightly inside it,
so the extracted volume is too low, by $1.5\%$ at resolution $24$, an
$O(h^2)$ error. A penalty that holds this volume at the exact value therefore
pushes the bubble to grow; Adam increases the distance between the centers as
fast as the radii, and the lobes separate into two balls, with
$A/\bar V^{2/3} = 9.6720$. In the ratio of \eqref{eq:bubbleloss} the same error
changes only the overall size, which the $w_s$ term fixes. Without the second
term, the first is minimized by collapsing one lobe, which gives $7.6766$; this
is below the equal-volume minimum $9.1394$ only because the volumes are no
longer equal.

The start is lopsided: $r_1/d = 0.75$ and $r_2/d = 0.57$, with
$d$ the distance between the centers, angles from $82^\circ$ to $146^\circ$,
and volumes $81\%$ apart. After $300$ Adam steps on a $32^3$ grid,
$r_1/d = r_2/d = 1.0064$ against the exact value $1$, the volumes agree to
$9.1\times10^{-7}$, the dihedral angles lie between $119.8^\circ$ and
$120.3^\circ$, and the objective is $9.1481$ against the minimum $9.1394$, a
relative error of $9.5\times10^{-4}$, and the final mesh is watertight. The
ratio $r/d$ and the angles measure the same condition. By the argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:plateau}, applied to the gradients of $f$, the three
normals at the triple curve have lengths $2r_1$, $2r_2$ and $2d$, so $r_1/d = r_2/d = 1$ holds exactly when the
three angles are equal. Refining the grid from $16$ to $48$ reduces the error
in $A/\bar V^{2/3}$ from $3.7\times10^{-3}$ to $4.2\times10^{-4}$ at a rate
of $1.98$, $|r_1/d - 1|$ from $2.3\times10^{-2}$ to $2.4\times10^{-3}$ at rate
$2.05$, and the worst deviation from $120^\circ$ from $1.24^\circ$ to
$0.13^\circ$ at rate $2.05$. Second-order convergence is the rate expected
from meshing a curved surface, so the remaining error is due to the
discretization.

\paragraph{Ablation: the same loss under case analysis.} We replace our
extractor by the corner-label extractor of Section~\ref{sec:failure} and keep
everything else: field family, loss, schedule, initialization and seed. It
converges to a different shape, with $r_1/d = r_2/d = 1.276$, angles between
$113.1^\circ$ and $133.9^\circ$, and $A/\bar V^{2/3} = 9.1886$ against our
$9.1481$. Its mesh is not watertight at any resolution we tested, and its
objective trace is noisy (Figure~\ref{fig:doublebubble}).

The baseline proposes features from the corner labels, locates them by
Newton iteration instead of a closed-form solve, solves against the field
instead of its interpolant, and runs without the perturbation. To find which
of these differences matters we ran two more extractors
(\suppp{sec:ablation-supp}). They keep the corner-label features but locate
them with our closed-form solves on the interpolant, one with the perturbation
and one without. Both match the baseline's vertex and triangle counts on test
power diagrams, both miss
the double bubble ($r_1/d = 0.765$ and $0.942$, against our $1.006$), and
neither is watertight. The case analysis alone is therefore enough to lose the
shape. To find the source of the noise, we score each trace by how much it
reverses direction relative to how fast it moves. The exact arrangement scores
$0.022$, the two new extractors $0.018$ and $0.023$, and the Newton baseline
$1.437$. The noise in Figure~\ref{fig:doublebubble} is therefore due to the
iterative solver.

Over five starts, the corner-label extractors end between $r_1/d = 0.74$ and $1.03$
with closed-form solves, between $0.70$ and $0.97$ without the perturbation,
and between $0.90$ and $2.87$ with Newton iteration; none of these fifteen
runs is watertight. Our method returns $1.006$ to $1.009$ from four of the
five starts and is watertight from all five. From the most lopsided start the
lobes separate into two balls, and once they are apart the gradient does not
bring them back together (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Refining the grid
does not change this. From resolution $16$ to $48$ the objective error of the baseline stays between $5.4\times10^{-3}$ and
$8.8\times10^{-3}$, two to thirteen times ours, and $|r_1/d - 1|$ takes the
values $0.20$, $0.05$, $0.28$ and $0.29$. The value $1.276$ above is therefore one sample from a wide
spread. The misread faces of Section~\ref{sec:failure} make the
loss nearly the same for nearby shapes, so where the optimizer stops depends
on which faces happen to be misread.

The minimizer lies in the field family by construction, so this experiment
tests the gradient through a triple curve rather than whether the field can
represent the answer (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Contact between three solid objects}
\label{sec:contact}

When solid objects touch, the contact area between each pair often has to be
measured, differentiated and driven to a prescribed value. Contact area
matters, for example, for the load carried between parts of an assembly and
for the heat conducted across an interface. If each object is meshed from its
own field, the contact set is lost (Section~\ref{sec:related}). We instead
write the objects as channels of one field. Each point then has one argmax
and hence one owner, so two objects cannot overlap, and the surface they
share is emitted once, as one set of triangles carrying their label pair
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). The material-interface mode of Du
et al.~\cite{du2022} returns the same contact geometry but not the derivative
that the optimization below requires. We use three balls of the field of
Section~\ref{sec:demos}, because three add a feature that two cannot produce;
the method places no limit on the number of objects.

The three balls meet along a curve that has no two-object
analog, and the curve ends at two quadruple points where the background joins
them. At resolution $48$ the extractor returns exactly two quadruple points,
and the curve between them has a relative error of $3.7\times10^{-3}$. Over
the refinement of Section~\ref{sec:demos} the curve converges at an average
order of $1.97$, the same rate as the wall area; Figure~\ref{fig:contact}
shows both, with the separation sweep.
\supp{sec:contact-supp} describes the configuration in full.

The optimization uses a different loss from \eqref{eq:bubbleloss}, with no
volume or size terms; it asks only for three prescribed contact areas,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i<j} \bigg(\frac{A_{ij} - A^\star_{ij}}{A^\star_{ij}}\bigg)^{\!2},
  \label{eq:contactloss}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ is the area of the wall between balls $i$ and $j$. The balls
start from a symmetric configuration with equal contact areas. The unequal
targets $A^\star_{ij}$ are measured on a second configuration at the same
resolution (\suppp{sec:contact-supp}), so they are known to be reachable. The loss reads only the shared
patches and needs no penalty term, margin or collision query, since no
configuration of the field puts two objects at the same point. After $140$
Adam steps on the centers and radii at $40^3$, all three areas match their
targets to within $6.5\times10^{-5}$ relative error.

The two-ball wall of Section~\ref{sec:demos} shows a
limit that applies to any objective defined on the contact set. For $s>2r$
there is no wall, so its area and gradient are zero, while just before
contact is lost the exact derivative is $-\pi r$. The contact area is
therefore continuous but not differentiable at the onset of contact, and the
extractor reproduces this without smoothing. The gradients can maintain and
reshape existing contact, but they cannot pull separated objects into contact.

\subsection{Clinical segmentation data}
\label{sec:realdata}

The input here is measured data: the per-class softmax volume of
TotalSegmentator's Couinaud liver-segment model~\cite{wasserthal2023} on a
thoracoabdominal CT. Eight segments tile the liver, so the curves where three
segments meet are part of the anatomy. A clinical segmentation has no ground
truth, so instead of an error we measure three properties of the output:
whether it is closed and consistently oriented, whether its vertices lie on
the field's own interface, and how its junctions compare with those of the
multi-label extractor in current tooling.

We use the $49^3$ window with the most cells that contain three
or more labels: a region of $38.6 \times 38.6 \times 72$\,mm at the native
spacing of the scan, $0.80$\,mm in plane and $1.5$\,mm between slices,
holding six labels. Distances in voxels are in units of $0.80$\,mm. The grid nodes coincide with the
voxel centers (to $2.1\times10^{-15}$), so the node values are the network's
own output, with no resampling. These values are probabilities, so the
partition is the argmax model of Hege et al.~\cite{hege1997}, which they
approximated by $6\times6\times6$ subsampling; we extract it exactly on the
$P_1$ interpolant. Interpolating the logits instead would give the same labels
at the nodes but a different partition between them, because the softmax is
not affine.

The baseline is \texttt{vtkSurfaceNets3D}~\cite{frisken2022, vtk}, which runs
a single multi-label pass, is threaded and fast, and shares points and cells
between adjacent regions so that no gaps form. The downstream filter
\texttt{vtkSurfaceNetsAtlas} groups that output into patches keyed by ordered
label pairs, the same format as Section~\ref{sec:assembly}. SurfaceNets takes
an integer label map, so we give it the argmax of the same window.

On $663\,552$ tetrahedra the extractor returns $69\,211$
triangles on $11$ distinct interfaces, with $920$ triple-curve segments and $2$
quadruple points, in $1.0$\,s (Figure~\ref{fig:realdata}b). The mesh is
closed and consistently oriented, and its vertices lie on the interface of
the interpolant they are computed from to a median of
$4.3\times10^{-16}$\,mm. Real data needs the perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} more than the synthetic fields do, because a
network output at finite precision repeats the same values across voxels. At
$\varepsilon = 0$ the vertices still lie within $6.0\times10^{-15}$\,mm of the
interface but closure fails; at $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$ closure holds and the
vertices lie within $5.0\times10^{-9}$\,mm of the unperturbed interface, eight
orders of magnitude below the voxel size.

SurfaceNets receives only labels, so it cannot place
the interface within a voxel. Against the same
interpolant its vertices lie a median of $0.115$\,mm ($0.14$ voxels) off the
interface, and $1.19$ voxels at the $99$th percentile. Since ours are
computed from that interpolant, we also measure both against one that neither
method uses, as in Hege et al.: the trilinear interpolant, which blends the
eight values around a cube, not the four of a tetrahedron. On the walls between segments our vertices
lie a median of $0.0095$ voxels from its interface and those of SurfaceNets
$0.090$, and the difference persists at the $90$th percentile.

Where the mesh would be non-manifold, SurfaceNets duplicates
points to keep it manifold, and its documentation notes that ``duplicated
points may diverge'' under smoothing. We identified the duplicates while they
still coincide and measured their separation on the smoothed mesh: $18$ points
in $6$ groups end up a mean of $1.09$ and at most $1.32$ voxels apart
(Figure~\sref{fig:realdatajunction} of the supplement). The mesh therefore
tears by more than a voxel where three regions meet. Our triple-curve vertices
are single shared indices, so the same separation is zero
(\suppp{sec:assembly-supp}).

This experiment is not an accuracy result;
the quantitative claims of this paper rest on the closed-form fields above.
The vertex-position comparison measures what rounding to integer labels
discards, a property of the input rather than of SurfaceNets. The tearing is
rare, six sites in ten thousand points, and occurs only at junctions.
\supp{sec:realdata-supp} gives the
per-vertex map, the $90$th percentiles and the boxed junction under both
methods.

No optimization is run on this data, but the output is
differentiable. The derivative of a vertex with respect to one class
probability at one voxel says how far the interface moves if the network
becomes more confident about that class there. TotalSegmentator is an nnU-Net,
a PyTorch network, so a loss on the mesh, such as the area of one interface or
the distance to a reference anatomy, could be back-propagated into its
weights; we do not train through it here.



\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}

In floating point the candidate sets are complete only up to the tolerance.
A region in which its label nowhere exceeds the others by more than
$\delta = 10^{-12}$ can be missed (Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}). Sampling
the interior of each tetrahedron at five points screens for missed labels,
and the screen flagged no field in this paper.

The perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:degeneracy} takes the place of
exact arithmetic. It moves vertices by $O(\varepsilon)$, cannot resolve
features closer than $\varepsilon$
(Section~\ref{sec:further}), can leave holes at three-way ties
(Section~\ref{sec:predicates}), and resolves exact ties differently from the
predicates of Du et al.~\cite{du2022} (Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}). Our
tests bound its error on a finite sample; their predicates guarantee
the correct structure on every input. The method is also slower
(Section~\ref{sec:headtohead}). Our cost
grows faster with the number of labels in a cell, and memory limits the
resolution (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

Accuracy on curved interfaces is limited by the $P_1$ interpolation of the
logits, not by the extraction. Projecting each vertex onto the interface of $f$
itself by a few Newton steps, differentiated through the implicit function theorem, would
separate the two. Since every vertex lies exactly on the interface of the
interpolant, the output also reproduces any noise in the field. FlexiCubes
averages the crossings of each cell into one vertex, which damps this noise,
and it optimizes a two-label field more accurately
(Section~\ref{sec:flexicubes}) and is the better choice for that task.
Reproducing prescribed region volumes, as volume-fraction reconstruction
requires, is a separate problem.

In the double-bubble and contact studies the answer lies in the field family
by construction, so they test whether descent through the extractor reaches
it; whether a given field model can express the answer is not tested. From the most lopsided of five
double-bubble starts the lobes separate into two balls and do not rejoin.
Contact gradients vanish while the objects are apart
(Section~\ref{sec:contact}), so they can reshape contact but not establish it;
an attraction term active within a margin is a possible, untested remedy.

The output consists of interfaces, not volume meshes. Passing it to an
unfitted or body-fitted solver, the motivating application in multi-material
engineering analysis~\cite{fromm2025, nife2024}, is left for future work, as is the fiber
computation of the cardiac pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:intro}.
Section~\ref{sec:realdata} uses liver segments because the cardiac model of
the same toolkit is not openly licensed.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented an extractor for the exact argmax partition of a
piecewise-linear multi-label field in which every vertex, including the
triple and quadruple points, is a closed-form linear solve. Each vertex is
therefore an explicit function of the nodal values, and reverse-mode automatic
differentiation carries exact gradients from a loss on the mesh to the field
and to the parameters that produced it. The patches are convex, the complex conforms
across cells without welding, and wherever the field is generic the
output agrees with the exact construction of Du et al.~\cite{du2022} on every
count. Corner-label case analysis, as in marching cubes, misplaces the triple
point on $40$ to $50\%$ of the three-label faces of two power diagrams at every
resolution tested; under the
double-bubble objective it converges to a different shape, while our
extractor recovers the double bubble with second-order convergence
(Section~\ref{sec:doublebubble}).

Because each point has exactly one label, the same operator makes contact
between solid objects differentiable. The objects cannot overlap, and a loss
on their shared surfaces alone reaches prescribed contact areas without a
penalty term. On a clinical segmentation network the output is closed and
has no duplicated junction vertices.
The method is slower than the exact-predicate construction, less accurate than
FlexiCubes when a two-label field is optimized, and replaces exact arithmetic
by a measured perturbation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
